# Arundo donax (giant reed)

Arundo donax (giant reed) is a tall perennial grass (family Poaceae) that typically forms dense stands on disturbed sites, sand dunes, riparian areas, and wetlands. It has invaded central and coastal Southern California river valleys, central California river valleys in San Luis Obispo and Monterey counties, the San Francisco Bay Area, the Sacramento and San Joaquin River valleys and is also increasing in the North Coast region. Arundo donax is threatening California’s riparian ecosystems by outcompeting native species, such as willows, reducing the nesting and foraging value of habitat, changing streamflow, and utilizing more water than native vegetation. It is native to northern Africa and western and central Asia.

## Identify

- **Form:** bamboo-like perennial grass, **up to 25 ft tall** — by far the tallest weedy grass in CA.
- **Canes:** erect, semi-woody, **1–2 in thick**. First-year canes are unbranched and green; older canes often branch with leaves only on the branches.
- **Leaves:** blade <3 ft long, 1–3 in wide. Ligule is a short minutely-fringed membrane 1–2 mm long. **Auricles and collar region distinctly pale yellowish-green** (field ID).
- **Rhizomes (management constraint):** creeping, thick, scaly, often forming a dense knotty network at stem bases. **Rhizome AND stem fragments with a node** can develop into new plants under wet conditions.
- **Inflorescences:** large terminal plume-like panicles, 1–2 ft long, silvery cream to purplish or brown.
- **Reproduction:** **does not produce viable seed in North America** — propagation is entirely vegetative from rhizomes and stem fragments. Dispersal: water, mud, human activity.
- **Habitat:** riparian corridors, floodplains, ditches — moist riparian sites. Doesn't survive prolonged freezing or arid conditions without water access.
- **Hazards:** highly flammable canes (year-round), increases riparian fire risk; uses 3× more water than native riparian vegetation; alternate host for several plant viruses.

## Remove

Default method: [`dig_rhizome_complete`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_rhizome_complete).

- **Critical:** **rhizome fragments left in the ground regrow.** Mechanical removal must extract the entire rhizome mass. Rhizomes buried under 3–10 ft of alluvium readily resprout.
- **Small infestations (<6 ft plants from new fragments):** hand-pull when soil is loose / wet. Hand-dig with cutting near the base.
- **Larger stands:** chainsaw or rotary brush cutter to reduce above-ground biomass, then mechanical removal of rhizomes (backhoe / excavator where terrain allows). Note that one-pass mechanical without herbicide leaves rhizome remnants that resprout.
- **Timing for cutting:** when plants begin to flower, when reserve energy in rhizomes is lowest.
- **Several cuttings** are needed before underground reserves exhaust; larger patches can resprout even after years of cutting alone.
- **Cut-stump glyphosate** (per WRIC, the gold standard): apply undiluted glyphosate with a paint brush within **1–2 minutes** after cutting the cane. **Excellent control, no resprouting**, in May / July / September trials.
- **Foliar glyphosate** (after burn or cut to ~6–8 ft of regrowth): 2–4 qt/acre or 2% v/v spot. Mid-summer to fall, after flowering and before dormancy. **Two to three years of treatment** typically needed.
- **Imazapyr + glyphosate** combination (Habitat 1 pt + glyphosate 1 qt/acre, fall) provides better control at lower cost than either alone.
- **Heavy equipment** on >30% slopes or saturated soils is unsafe; not a universal option.
- **Cane disposal:** chip or burn on site — rhizome and stem fragments left lying around will regenerate if moisture is present.
- **Goats** (especially Angora and Spanish goats) can graze giant reed and access slopes too steep for machines.
- **Flamethrower / weed burner** spot treatment can heat-girdle stems at the base — useful in wet weather when fire risk is low.

## Prevent

- **Don't move soil from infested sites.** Even a single rhizome fragment in fill dirt or equipment tires can establish a new stand.
- **Don't compost or pile cut canes** — every node can root in moist soil.
- **Plan for 2–3 years of follow-up herbicide** at minimum; large stands take longer.
- **Downstream watch:** flood events disperse rhizome and stem fragments downstream — track new infestations along the watercourse for years after upstream control.
- **Replant cleared riparian areas** with competitive native riparian vegetation; bare ground is reinvaded quickly by stem fragments transported by floods.

## Why it's a problem

**Q1.1:** High water use and light suppression of competing species. Water temperature increase due to reduced shading typical of native trees. Can form near monotypic stands.

**Q1.2:** Displaces native vegetation, shades out lower level plant species. Has been show to comprise 68% of riparian vegetation in Santa Ana River. Other observations indicate nearly pure monotypic stands.
