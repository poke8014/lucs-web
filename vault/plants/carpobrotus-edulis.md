# Carpobrotus edulis (highway iceplant)

Carpobrotus edulis (Hottentot-fig or iceplant) is a succulent shrub (family Aizoaceae) found throughout coastal California and the Channel Islands, especially in areas with a warm winter climate. Native to South Africa and introduced as an ornamental plant, Carpobrotus edulis now inhabits coastal scrub, grasslands, chaparral, bluffs, dunes and beaches where it creates dense mats that increase soil organic matter over time, allowing new non-native species to invade. Carpobrotus edulis propagates by seed and vegetatively. Even small stem fragments can regenerate into a new plant, making control difficult.

## Identify

- **Form:** mat-forming or trailing perennial succulent, to 1.5 ft tall. Single clonal patches can reach **~200 ft in diameter**.
- **Leaves (key field ID):** **succulent, sessile, opposite, elongate, triangular in cross-section** — like a thick three-sided pickle. Bright green; turn reddish when stressed.
- **Stems:** woody near the base, trail up to 10 ft, **root at every node** along the way.
- **Flowers:** showy, solitary at stem tips, 8–10 cm diameter, with numerous **pink or yellow (aging to pink) linear petals**.
- **Fruit:** large fleshy berry-like capsules that persist on plants for months, eventually yellowing.
- **Reproduction:** **both vegetative (stem fragments)** and **seed**. Numerous seeds per capsule; seeds disperse via animals (deer, rabbits, rodents) that consume the fruit. **Seeds passed through animal guts germinate better than from intact fruit.** Uneaten fruit go dormant ~3 years until they decompose.
- **Habitat:** coastal scrub, grassland, chaparral, bluffs, dunes, sandy coastal sites. Does not tolerate cold winters.
- **Ecological harm:** displaces native dune species; **traps sand and stabilizes dunes unnaturally** (native dune systems require flux). Accumulates organic matter in sandy soil, promoting invasion by additional weed species. **Plants die when completely buried** — useful for management.
- **Confused with:** *Carpobrotus chilensis* (sea fig, Cal-IPC Moderate) — both are iceplants but with different leaf shapes and flower colors.

## Remove

Default method: [`sheet_mulch_smother`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#sheet_mulch_smother) for established mats; mechanical removal is also effective.

- **Mechanical removal is effective any time of year.** Iceplants are easily hand-pulled — tear up by the roots.
- **The "carpet rolling" technique:** large mats can be removed by rolling them up like a carpet. Labor-intensive but very effective and selective.
- **Critical:** **the plant can grow roots and shoots from any node**, so **all live plants and stem fragments must be removed from soil contact** to prevent resprouting.
- **Mulching with the removed plant material on top of the cleared soil** can be adequate to prevent most resprouting — leave the mat upside-down to dry, but plan one follow-up visit to remove resprouts.
- **Heavy equipment** (scoop with brush rake, skid-steer, bobcat) works for large infestations but **still requires removing buried stems and mulching the soil**.
- **Tarping** (clear or black plastic) blocks light and eventually kills the plant — a slower passive alternative for large patches.
- **Burying** — completely covering with soil kills the plant. Useful tactic where dune restoration involves replacing displaced sand.
- **Foliar herbicides:**
  - **Glyphosate** 1.3 qt/acre or 1.5–2% v/v spot — the only chemical option registered in California shown to effectively kill *Carpobrotus*. Apply when plant is rapidly growing. **Shielded sprayer or 50% wiper application** is recommended to minimize off-target damage.
  - 1% surfactant added to glyphosate increases effectiveness.
- **Don't burn** — heat kills seeds but the succulent foliage won't combust enough to carry a burn.
- **Don't graze** — particularly on sensitive dune systems.
- **No biocontrol** agents available; iceplant scale insects (*Pulvinariella* spp.) have minor impact but aren't a useful tool.

## Prevent

- **After removal, watch for an organic debris layer.** The accumulated dead plant material contains iceplant seeds and other weed seeds; the carbon enriches the sandy soil. **Remove the debris layer too** if doing native dune restoration, or plant competitive native dune species immediately.
- **Animals disperse the fruit** — eradicating one patch doesn't eliminate the seed source from neighboring infestations.
- **Highway plantings are the legacy spread vector** — Caltrans planted iceplant extensively along coastal highways for erosion control decades ago. Many roadside infestations are unmaintained remnants of those plantings.
- **Don't replant cleared dunes with non-native ornamentals** — dunes need their natural sand-flux dynamics; native dune restoration is the right replacement.

## Why it's a problem

**Q1.1:** Reduces soil pH and influences nutrient dynamics (1).

**Q1.2:** Dense fibrous root system interferes with water uptake by other plants. Native shrubs increased in canopy size when iceplant was removed (1). Outcompetes grasses
