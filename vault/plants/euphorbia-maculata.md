# Euphorbia maculata (spotted spurge)

Spotted spurge (*Euphorbia maculata*) is a prostrate summer annual (family Euphorbiaceae) native to the eastern United States. In California it's the most common of the weedy spurges; it invades thin turf, ornamental beds, vegetable gardens, container plants, and sidewalk cracks. **All weedy spurges share the same management playbook**, so this page covers spotted spurge as the primary species and notes the close relatives — creeping spurge (*E. serpens*), petty spurge (*E. peplus*), ground spurge (*E. prostrata*), garden spurge (*E. hirta*), nodding spurge (*E. nutans*), and thyme-leafed spurge (*E. serpyllifolia*). Broken stems exude a **milky sap that's a skin / eye irritant** and is **toxic to grazing animals** — the chief safety consideration when working with this plant.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7445 (updated 09/2009). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification

- **Form:** low-growing prostrate annual forming **dense mats up to 3 ft in diameter**.
- **Leaves (field ID):** dark green, paired (opposite), 1/8–1/2 in long, 1/8 in wide. **A red spot frequently marks the leaf halfway down its center vein** — this is the diagnostic feature for *E. maculata*. Ground spurge (*E. prostrata*) is similar but **never has the red spot**.
- **Sap (the safety lever):** stems, leaves, and broken parts secrete a **milky white sap**. The sap is a skin and eye irritant for most people; it's toxic to sheep at as little as 0.62% of body weight.
- **Hairs:** stems, fruit, and leaves are hairy.
- **Stipules:** tiny scalelike appendages at the base of each leaf — need a 10× hand lens to see.
- **Flowers:** tiny pinkish flowers in small cup-like structures (cyathia) in the leaf axils. Easily missed.
- **Taproot:** central taproot capable of extending **24+ in into soil**, supporting drought tolerance once established.
- **Distinguishing the spurges:** the key in the UC IPM Pest Note separates them by flower cluster shape, stem habit (erect vs prostrate, rooting at nodes or not), hair pattern, seed surface texture, and presence/absence of the red leaf spot. Creeping spurge (*E. serpens*) is the only spurge that also roots at the nodes — making it harder to remove than the others.

## Biology

- **Summer annual.** Germinates best at 75–85°F; possible 60–100°F. Germination needs **light** — seeds buried >1/2 in won't germinate well.
- **Germination window:** February through September wherever moisture is available in California. Cool early-spring seedlings stay small until summer warmth triggers fast growth.
- **Seed production:** thousands of seeds per plant. Seeds produced in summer germinate immediately; seeds produced in late fall mostly stay dormant until spring.
- **Reproductive timeline:** flowers and seed within **5 weeks of germination** — fast turnover means waiting until you see flowering plants is already late.
- **Creeping spurge exception:** *E. serpens* also reproduces vegetatively by rooting at stem nodes — treat it more like [[plants/hedera-helix]] (every node a propagule) than like other annual spurges.

## Remove

Default method: [`hand_pull`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#hand_pull).

- **Wear gloves** — milky sap is a skin / eye irritant.
- **Pull young plants before they set seed (within 5 weeks of germination).** Plants often break at the stem when hand-pulled, leaving root and several buds — these regrow. Get the taproot when possible.
- **Mowing doesn't work** — plants grow flat to the ground below the blade.
- **Mulch (2+ in organic, or landscape fabric with bark on top)** is the most effective non-chemical control. Spurge seeds need light; thick mulch starves them. Coarse bark needs **3–4 in** to be effective.
- **Solarization** is highly effective: clear plastic over moist soil for 4–6 weeks during July–August summer heat kills the seedbank in the top few inches. **Don't cultivate afterward** — that brings up deeper seed. Best in inland areas where summer temps exceed 90°F; partial control in cooler coastal sites.
- **Don't bring container plants with spurge into clean beds** — nursery transplants are a common arrival vector. Inspect transplants and remove visible plants before installing.
- **In turf:** maintain dense competitive grass. Raise mowing height to **2+ in** in tall fescue or perennial ryegrass to reduce invasions. Keep thatch under 1/2 in.
- **Postemergent herbicides** (home-gardener available): 2,4-D / MCPP / dicamba combinations, triclopyr (Turflon), glyphosate. **2,4-D doesn't control larger, mature plants** — apply early when plants are still seedlings. Glyphosate is the broad-spectrum spot-spray option in non-turf areas.
- **Preemergents** (apply before soil reaches 55–60°F in late winter): home-gardener-available products include pendimethalin, trifluralin, dithiopyr, and oryzalin. Oryzalin + benefin combinations are available retail. Isoxaben (Gallery) is professional-only.
- **No effective biocontrol** — insects feed on spurges but no specialized controls have been developed.

## Prevent

- **Prevention is the primary management strategy.** Once spurge establishes, it's very hard to dislodge because of the fast germination-to-seed cycle (5 weeks) and 2-year-plus seed viability.
- **Use weed-free planting stock and seed.**
- **Clean mowers, clothes, and tools** after working in infested areas — small seeds cling readily.
- **Don't move soil from infested sites** without solarizing or sifting.
- **Watch sidewalk cracks and bare-soil edges** — spurge invades exactly the marginal spaces dense planting hasn't filled.
