# Hedera helix (English ivy)

Hedera helix (English ivy) is a perennial evergreen woody vine (family Araliaceae). It is found throughout California along the coast, as well as in Shasta and Butte Counties. English ivy grows vigorously in forests where nothing else seems able to compete and inhibits regeneration of understory plants, including forest wildflowers and new trees and shrubs. It is native to most of Europe and western Asia.

## Identify

- **Form:** fast-growing perennial evergreen woody vine. Can live **100+ years**.
- **Two growth forms — this matters for ID:**
  - **Juvenile** (what most people encounter as ground cover or climbing a wall): viny stems to 12 in long, leaves usually three-lobed (occasionally five-lobed), up to 12 in wide. Stems develop adventitious roots along the ground and aerial root-like structures that cling to bark / masonry.
  - **Adult reproductive form:** erect, shrubby, non-climbing. Leaves ovate-to-diamond-shaped (no lobes), up to 6 in. Plants must reach adult form to flower — juvenile phase can last 10+ years.
- **Leaves:** smooth upper surface, often slightly glossy, usually dark green. Leaf stalks and lower leaf surfaces are sometimes glabrous but **usually covered with grayish star-shaped hairs**.
- **Flowers:** in **fall** — unusual for CA (most natives flower spring/summer). Racemes or panicles of simple umbels.
- **Fruit:** berry-like, dark blue to black, 4–8 mm wide, ripening in spring. A mature plant produces **tens of thousands of fruit per year**, bird-dispersed.
- **Reproduction:** by seed (adult plants) and **vegetatively from juvenile stem fragments** — any stem fragment in contact with moist soil can regenerate.
- **Hedera helix vs Algerian ivy (H. canariensis):** Algerian has larger, less-lobed, glossier leaves; H. helix has the typical small three-lobed leaves. Both are invasive; WRIC and Cal-IPC treat them together.
- **Skin warning:** sap causes contact dermatitis in ~10% of the population. Berries and leaves are toxic to humans and cattle if ingested in quantity.

## Remove

Default method: [`pull_vine_dig_crown`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#pull_vine_dig_crown).

- **Ground cover:** pull individual stems off the ground; **it is essential to remove all runners** — every node re-roots.
- **Stem fragments regenerate from soil contact.** If off-site removal isn't possible, place plant parts off the ground in a way that lets them dry out. Do not leave cut debris on moist soil.
- **Mowing or cutting alone is not recommended** — fragments scatter and regenerate. This contradicts the common landscaper "mow it back first" recommendation and is a **material WRIC delta** worth surfacing in user-facing copy.
- **Climbing trees:**
  - Cut the climbing stems at ground level with a saw, then cut again at chest height; pry vines off the bark if loose, or leave them to die in place and fall in the next hot/dry period.
  - Don't try to rip live aerial vines off — embedded aerial roots can damage the bark; tree weight from accumulated vines makes the tree wind-susceptible regardless.
  - Occasionally vines are embedded in the trunk and cannot be removed cleanly.
- **Older plants** generally do not resprout from roots; **younger plants** do — be more thorough digging out young plants.
- **Cut-stump herbicide** (paint within minutes of cutting):
  - Triclopyr amine (Garlon 3A) → undiluted, or 33% in water.
  - Triclopyr ester (Garlon 4 Ultra) → 20% v/v in water.
  - Glyphosate 41% → 25% v/v in water.
  - **Stem injection ("drill and fill")** for large mature plants climbing trees: 100% Garlon 3A or Garlon 4 Ultra into drilled holes.
- **Foliar spray** (active growth, late summer / early fall):
  - Triclopyr 2–5% v/v + surfactant.
  - Glyphosate 2–4% v/v.
  - Coverage should thoroughly wet the leaves.
- **Spraying after string-trimming most leaves** has also proved successful (per WRIC).
- **Blowtorch / propane flame weeder** can work but must be **repeated** until reserves are exhausted — single-pass burning is not effective.

## Prevent

- **Gloves and long sleeves are standard.** Sap dermatitis affects ~10% of the population.
- **Bag cuttings; don't compost.** Stems survive home compost piles and re-root in the finished pile.
- **Watch tree-climbing infestations.** Trees with significant ivy cover become wind-susceptible from the added weight; remove ivy *from trees* even when you're not removing ground cover.
- **Old homesteads and abandoned yards** are persistent seed sources — landscape-scale prevention requires removing adult / fruiting plants from the neighborhood, not just your own yard.
- **Hedera is still widely sold** in CA garden centers ([UC IPM Invasive Plants PN](../raw/articles/ucipm-general/invasive-plants.md)). Voluntary nursery avoidance is the primary prevention pathway — the [PlantRight program](https://plantright.org/) tracks regional alternatives.

## Why it's a problem

**Q1.1:** C; Increased erosion in stream channels. By suppressing seedling establishment of woody understory shrubs dense stands of ivy can reduce the stability of stream banks that are stabalized by these deeply rooted plants.

**Q1.2:** A; Completely replace native vegetation, including trees, shrubs and herbaceous plants. Ivy largely eliminates the ability of other plants to reproduce by outcompeting their seedlings for light. In addition this plant smothers existing shrubs and trees by climbing and finally completely enshrouding them.
