# Urtica urens (burning nettle) and Urtica dioica (stinging nettle)

UC IPM's Pest Note covers two *Urtica* species together because the **stinging-hair safety hazard** is the same for both, even though the biology differs. **Burning nettle (*Urtica urens*)** is a small-to-medium-size **summer annual** non-native broadleaf — the species typically found in residential gardens. **Stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*)** is a tall **perennial** broadleaf that's **native to North America and California**, often growing in colonies along creeks, riverbanks, and moist uncultivated areas. Both have **fine bristly hairs that inject irritating substances on contact** — reddish patches, itching, burning, sometimes 12+ hours of tingling. Unlike poison-oak (which only affects allergic people), nettles cause irritant dermatitis in everyone. This page uses *U. urens* as the primary slug since it's the more common garden weed; *U. dioica* is the native congener — remove only where it's a safety or access problem near pasture, paths, or play areas.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 74146 (updated 10/2008). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification

### Burning nettle (*Urtica urens*) — non-native annual

- **Form:** small-to-medium summer annual, **5 in to 2 ft tall**, slender upright with sparse four-angled stems.
- **Cotyledons:** bright green, notched at the tips, smooth along edges.
- **Leaves:** opposite, stalked, **toothed margins**, 1/2–2 in long, with 3–5 veins radiating from the base. **Stinging and non-stinging hairs on leaf blade and stalk.**
- **Flowers:** ~2/5 in long, greenish-white, in clusters; male and female flowers in the same cluster.
- **Fruit:** small (1/16–1/10 in), triangular, single-seeded.
- **Germination:** seeds germinate late fall through early spring. Plants flower January–April typically; year-round along the California coast. Annual lifecycle — plant dies within one year.

### Stinging nettle (*Urtica dioica*) — native perennial

- **Form:** tall perennial broadleaf, **3.5–10 ft tall** (up to 20 ft in some situations). Often in colonies.
- **Cotyledons:** round-to-oval, hairless except for a few stinging hairs.
- **Leaves:** opposite, 2.5–5 in long, coarsely toothed, lance-shaped, with 3–5 veins radiating from the base. **Both stinging and non-stinging hairs on leaf surfaces.**
- **Stems:** angular, branched from the base, with long stinging hairs plus short non-stinging hairs.
- **Flowers:** separate male and female clusters at leaf bases, whitish-green and inconspicuous.
- **Fruit:** ~1/25 in, egg-shaped.
- **Germination:** seeds germinate in spring. **Rhizome fragments also develop into mature plants** — major management lever vs. burning nettle.
- **Habitat:** moist uncultivated areas — riverbanks, fence rows, roadsides, creek edges. Not generally a garden or row-crop weed.

## How the stinging works

The prickly hairs are tiny tubelike structures with a hard round bulb at the tip and a softer vessel at the base. **Contact breaks the bulb and exposes a needle-like point** that penetrates skin and injects the basal vessel's contents — neurotransmitters including acetylcholine and histamine. Effect is **irritant dermatitis (affects everyone)**, not allergic dermatitis (which affects only a subset, as with poison-oak). Reactions: red itchy patches, burning sensation, tingling that can persist 12+ hours.

## Remove

Default method: [`hand_pull`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#hand_pull) for burning nettle; [`dig_rhizome_complete`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_rhizome_complete) for stinging nettle.

- **Wear gloves and long sleeves.** Non-negotiable for both species. Skin irritation is universal, not allergy-dependent.
- **Burning nettle:** pull or hoe annual plants before they set seed. Hand-pulling the annual species is straightforward with gloves on.
- **Stinging nettle:** remove the entire **rhizome**, or the plant regrows. Repeated removal works best — **don't cultivate casually**, since chopping rhizomes can spread the patch.
- **Stinging nettle is native:** only control where it causes economic or safety problems (near pastures, trails, play areas). Otherwise let it stand — it's a valued native species, host to several butterflies and a traditional food/medicinal plant for humans.
- **Close mowing** prevents fruit development but doesn't kill the plants. Repeated cultivation works on stinging nettle if persistent across multiple seasons.
- **Herbicides** (all licensed-applicator-only for nettles): isoxaben, oxadiazon, oxyfluorfen. Not generally available to home gardeners.

## Prevent

- **Gloves + long sleeves + closed shoes** are the routine PPE when working near or removing nettles. Sting symptoms can last 12+ hours.
- **Stinging nettle hosts native butterflies** (red admiral, satyr comma, Milbert's tortoiseshell) — for naturalistic gardens, leaving a stinging-nettle patch in a remote moist corner is a legitimate native-plant choice rather than removal.
- **Don't compost nettle in livestock-accessible areas** without thorough drying — sting hairs persist on dried plant material.
- **Both species are edible when cooked** (sting hairs are inactivated by steaming/boiling) — relevant for foraging gardens; not a control method.
