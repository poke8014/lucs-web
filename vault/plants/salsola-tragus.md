# Salsola tragus (Russian thistle / tumbleweed)

Russian thistle (*Salsola* spp. — *S. tragus* is the most common in California) is the iconic Western "tumbleweed." A summer annual native to Eurasia, first introduced to South Dakota in 1873 as a flax-seed contaminant; now found in every state except Alaska and Florida. The mature plant **detaches at the base in fall, then tumbles miles in the wind** scattering seeds — a single plant can produce 200,000 seeds. **Seed viability is short (90%+ either germinate or decay in the first year)**, so persistent prevention of seed set substantially clears infestations. **Accumulated tumbleweeds along fence lines, against buildings, and in irrigation canals are a serious fire hazard** — prairie wildfires can spread quickly via burning tumbleweed embers. Russian thistle is rarely a problem in managed gardens or turf; it thrives in disturbed loose soil that other plants can't compete in.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7486 (updated 05/2025). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification

- **Form:** bushy summer annual with many slender stems, often **reddish-to-purplish striped**. Stems 8–36 in. Mature plant 18 in to 6+ ft in diameter.
- **Seedlings:** very finely dissected leaves that look almost like **pine needles**.
- **Young plant leaves:** fleshy, dark green, narrow, ~1 in long.
- **Mature leaves (key field ID):** **short, stiff, with a sharp-pointed tip** — the source of the "thistle" common name. Inconspicuous flowers (no petals) borne above pairs of spine-tipped bracts in leaf axils.
- **Detachment behavior:** after frost, plant dies and base of stem becomes brittle. **Breaks off at soil level in fall/early winter** and tumbles in wind, dispersing seeds across miles. Plant cannot survive a hard frost.
- **Seed germination:** rapid — seed contains a fully formed embryo that uncoils as it germinates. Taproot extends within ~12 hours. **Requires loose soil** — won't germinate well in firm soil. Late winter through early spring typically; can extend into summer/fall with adequate moisture.
- **Seed viability:** **90%+ either germinate or decay in the first year**; only a fraction survive longer than 2 years. Short seedbank is the management lever.
- **Habitat:** disturbed loose soil — roadsides, fence lines, vacant lots, abandoned gardens, agricultural fields. Especially well adapted to desert and alkaline conditions. **Rarely invades managed gardens or turf.**
- **Hazards:**
  - **Skin irritation** — many people develop rashes and allergic reactions from handling the plant.
  - **Pollen allergen** during summer.
  - **Spiky mature leaves** poke when stepping or working without gloves.
  - **Fire hazard** when accumulated as dried tumbleweeds against structures, fences, vegetation.
  - **Toxic to livestock** when mature — nitrates and oxalates accumulate.

## Remove

Default method: [`hand_pull`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#hand_pull) at early growth.

- **Pull young plants by hand or with tools** — easy when small, before stems lignify and leaves get spiny.
- **At later growth stages, wear gloves** — plant material causes skin irritation and the spines hurt.
- **Mow at first bloom** to prevent seed set; **time it carefully** — too early and plants regrow, too late and seeds form. Repeat as needed.
- **Don't disturb the soil in already-vacant areas** — loose soil is what Russian thistle needs to germinate. Avoid discing or unnecessary tilling. **Plant competitive desirable vegetation** in disturbed lots and patios to deny the niche.
- **Dispose of mature tumbleweeds** trapped against fences and structures **before they break loose** — clearing them in fall prevents next year's seed dispersal and removes the fire hazard.
- **Burning** can dispose of accumulated dried plants but many seeds will have already dispersed. **Flame weeding** (propane torch / flame weeder) on seedlings and small rosettes is effective if done right after rain when humidity is high and dry vegetation has wetted — never with dry surroundings.
- **Heavy mulch** blocks light from reaching the soil and prevents germination.
- **Biocontrol:** introduced leaf-mining and stem-boring moths exist (*Coleophora klimeschiella*, *C. parthenica*) but predators keep them from being effective. Research ongoing on a blister mite (*Aceria salsolae*) and a stem-boring caterpillar (*Gymnancyla canella*) — years from release.
- **Preemergent herbicides** (home-gardener available): isoxaben, oryzalin, pendimethalin, trifluralin. Apply to bare soil in late winter / early spring before seed germinates.
- **Postemergent herbicides** (home-gardener):
  - **Broadleaf-selective for use near turf:** 2,4-D, dicamba, triclopyr. Apply early in the season when plants are tender; **late-season spiny plants resist herbicides**.
  - **Nonselective for spot spray:** glyphosate, pelargonic acid. Best on small plants.
- **Herbicide resistance:** Russian thistle has evolved resistance to chlorsulfuron, sulfometuron, and glyphosate within a few years of repeated single-herbicide use. **Rotate modes of action** in any chemical program.

## Prevent

- **Maintain dense desirable vegetation** in fence lines, parking strips, and abandoned corners of the yard — Russian thistle competes poorly with established plants. It's largely a weed of bare, loose, disturbed soil.
- **Clear tumbleweeds before they accumulate** along fences, against buildings, or in wood piles — they're a serious fire hazard, especially in dry-fall fire weather.
- **Keep livestock away from infested pastures** in late season — mature plants accumulate nitrates and oxalates.
- **Don't disturb dry soil unnecessarily** — disking compacted vacant lots creates exactly the niche Russian thistle thrives in.
- **Short seedbank (1–2 years) is the long-term lever** — two consecutive years of preventing seed set substantially clears an infestation.
