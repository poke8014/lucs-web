# Polygonum aviculare (common knotweed)

Common knotweed (*Polygonum aviculare*, often listed as *P. arenastrum* in the management literature) is a prostrate annual (family Polygonaceae) found along paths, walkways, gravel parking areas, and any spot with **compacted soil**. The presence of common knotweed is a soil-compaction indicator — it thrives where other plants can't, including sidewalk cracks and athletic field sidelines. Stems form prostrate mats 3–4 ft in diameter; the deep taproot (18+ in) lets it survive California summer drought. The first-order management move isn't herbicide, it's **fixing the underlying soil compaction**.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7484 (updated 08/2008). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification

- **Form:** prostrate annual (occasionally short-lived perennial) with **slender wiry stems highly branched** to form a flat mat. In cultivated conditions can grow erect to 4–8 in.
- **Leaves:** bluish-green, narrowly ovate, 1/5–4/5 in long. Short leaf stalks.
- **Stem nodes (key field ID):** slightly swollen, encircled by **papery leaf stipules** — the "knot"-like nodes the common name comes from.
- **Taproot:** single, penetrates **18+ in** deep — drives the drought tolerance.
- **Flowers:** small, inconspicuous, in upper leaf axils. White-to-green, sometimes pinkish.
- **Seed:** three-sided dark-brown achene, ~1/8 in. Long-lived in soil.
- **Distinguish from spotted spurge:** spotted spurge exudes **milky white sap** from broken stems; knotweed does not. Same prostrate growth form otherwise.
- **Distinguish from silver-sheathed knotweed (*Polygonum argyrocoleon*):** silver-sheathed is more erect (12–20 in), has long leafless rose-colored flower spikes, and shiny seeds.
- **Habitat:** compacted soil — turf, paths, gravel parking, sports field sidelines, foot paths. Germinates in late winter/early spring; flowers March–October.

## Remove

Default method: [`hand_pull`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#hand_pull).

- **Aerate or loosen compacted soil** — the highest-leverage move, because knotweed only thrives where compaction has killed off competitors. **In lawns, aerate then overseed with locally adapted grass.**
- **Reduce traffic** with fences or hedges; install rock or pavement where foot traffic is unavoidable; rotate heavily-used athletic-field positions.
- **Hand-pull or hoe** with a swivel hoe — easy to remove because the taproot lifts cleanly when soil is moist. Effective in home gardens.
- **Mulch (3–4 in coarse organic, or landscape fabric + bark/rock)** prevents seedling establishment. Reapply yearly as it degrades. **Avoid finely-shredded mulch** — seeds germinate in it.
- **Prevent seed set** on young plants to deplete the soil seed reserve over years.
- **Preemergent herbicides** (home-use): benefin, dithiopyr, oryzalin, pendimethalin, prodiamine, trifluralin. Apply before seeds germinate.
- **Postemergent herbicides** on young plants (under 3 in diameter): glyphosate, pelargonic acid, 2,4-D. Dicamba in turf only. **Mature plants** sometimes regrow from crown buds after top-kill — repeat treatments.

## Prevent

- **Treat knotweed as a symptom, not the cause.** Compacted soil is the upstream problem; managing the compaction prevents reinfestation.
- **Spread foot and vehicle traffic** across a broader area rather than concentrating wear on a path.
- **In lawns, aerate and overseed** compacted spots to give desirable grass a competitive shot.
- **Knotweed is a host for parasitic dodder (*Cuscuta*)** and some plant diseases (powdery mildew) — extra reason to keep it out of vegetable beds.
- **Flowers attract some predatory beneficial insects** — minor ecological benefit, not enough to outweigh the weediness in residential settings.
