# Convolvulus arvensis (field bindweed)

Field bindweed (*Convolvulus arvensis*) is a hardy perennial vine (family Convolvulaceae) native to Eurasia, first documented in California in 1884. By the 1920s it had been proclaimed the worst weed in California and most other western states. Roots can reach **20 ft deep** and seed can stay viable in soil for up to **60 years** — combined with prostrate-to-climbing stems that twine through shrubs and ornamentals, it's one of the most persistent residential weeds in the state.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7462 (updated 10/2011). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identify

- **Form:** prostrate, trailing perennial unless given something to climb on. When it finds shrubs, vegetables, or grapevines, stems twine up through the foliage with flowers exposed to the light above.
- **Seedling cotyledons:** nearly square with a shallow notch at the tip — distinctive vs. annual morningglory (*Ipomoea* spp.) cotyledons. Plants arising from rhizomes lack seed leaves entirely.
- **Mature leaves:** **arrowhead-shaped**, 1/2–2 in long, on a petiole that's flattened and grooved on top.
- **Flowers (field ID):** trumpet-shaped, white-to-pink, 1–1.5 in wide. Distinguishable from annual morningglory by size — *Ipomoea* flowers are ~2 in wide and the stems are thicker / sometimes hairy with heart-shaped (not arrowhead) leaves.
- **Roots (the management constraint):** **vertical roots reach 20+ ft deep**; 70% of root mass is in the top 2 ft. Lateral roots run ~1 ft deep, then turn down 15–30 in from the parent to form secondary vertical roots and new shoots. A single plant can spread radially **10+ ft in a growing season**. **Root and rhizome fragments as short as 2 in form new plants.**
- **Seed:** 1–4 seeds per round, smooth, 1/4-in capsule. ~550 seeds per plant. Seed coat is impervious to water within a month of maturity, so seeds survive **60+ years** in soil before scarification.
- **Drought tolerance:** prefers heavy clay; survives sprinkler, drip, or no summer water. Cuts seed production first when water-stressed but keeps growing.

## Remove

Default method: [`dig_rhizome_complete`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_rhizome_complete) — but treat it like [[plants/lepidium-latifolium]]: the rhizome network is so extensive that mechanical-only control rarely succeeds in a single season. Plan multi-year.

- **Seedlings are easy** to cultivate or hand-pull for the first 3–4 weeks after germination. After that, perennial root buds form and removal becomes much harder.
- **Repeated cultivation alone** (every 2–3 weeks during the growing season) reduces vigor but rarely eradicates. Pair with withholding water in summer to drive the roots into stress — assuming the roots haven't tapped deep moisture.
- **Tillage often spreads the infestation** — 2-inch root fragments regenerate, and one-pass tillage scatters them.
- **Light-blocking covers** (landscape fabric, black plastic, cardboard + organic mulch) are one of the most effective non-chemical controls — but **edges must overlap completely** because bindweed stems will find any seam and grow into the light. Complete death under the mulch takes **3–5 years**. Once the cover comes off, new seedlings will emerge from the 60-year seedbank, so plan for ongoing control afterward.
- **Glyphosate** at 2% v/v on actively growing plants (no moisture stress) is the standard residential chemical option. **No residual activity** — repeat applications needed. Glyphosate paints onto leaves work in shrub beds; rest the bindweed on a piece of newspaper to keep the herbicide off desirable plants while it dries.
- **Lawn-safe option:** 2,4-D and/or dicamba in turf. Field bindweed isn't usually a problem in healthy mowed turf, but established patches need repeated 2,4-D / dicamba applications during the growing season.
- **Preemergents** (trifluralin, oryzalin, pendimethalin, prodiamine) suppress germinating seedlings in ornamental beds but **don't kill established plants**. Pair with glyphosate / mulch to address the perennial roots.
- **Best timing for systemic herbicides:** fall when bindweed is actively growing — herbicide moves into the root system. Spring application also helps by reducing that year's seed production.

## Prevent

- **Don't introduce contaminated topsoil, planting stock, or seed.** Field bindweed often arrives in nursery containers or fill dirt.
- **Catch new seedlings in the first month.** A small patch becomes a yard-wide problem in one season because of the radial root spread.
- **No single-season fix exists.** Plan an **integrated multi-year program**: prevent seed production → reduce stored carbohydrates by deep tillage / repeated cutting → shade-out with competitive plantings or mulch → herbicide on regrowth. Constant vigilance is the work.
- **Bindweed grows through landscape fabric** if there are holes for plants or gaps at the edges. Plan the cover layout assuming the bindweed will find every seam.
