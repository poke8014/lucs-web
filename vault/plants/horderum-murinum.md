# Hordeum murinum (hare barley)

Hordeum murinum (hare barley or foxtail) is an annual grass (family Poaceae) that is native to the Mediterranean, northern Africa, and temperate Asia. It is characterized by having a low stature (<=10 cm), inflorescences with long awns, and spikelets (each containing three florets) that disarticulate when they mature. Hare barely is common throughout California in disturbed areas and can persist even with frequent mowing by growing nearly flat. It can outcompete native perennials by growing into bare areas and prohibiting their establishment, is salt tolerant, and may increase fire risk by adding fine fuels. Spikelets can lodge in nasal passages, throats etc. of animals. Hare barley may have arrived in California with Spanish settlers and is more common than its near relative, Mediterranean barley ( Hordeum marinum) in disturbed, dry upland areas.

## Identify

- **Form:** cool-season annual grass; mature plants 1–3 ft tall (typically 1–2). Can grow nearly flat when frequently mowed.
- **Auricles (key field ID):** **well-developed auricles that clasp the stem** — this is the distinction from Mediterranean barley (*Hordeum marinum* ssp. *gussonianum*), which lacks auricles or has very short ones (<2 mm).
- **Leaves:** flat, narrow (3–8 mm), typically hairy.
- **Stems:** round, erect to spreading, **often bend abruptly at the base**.
- **Inflorescence:** bristly thick spike 1–3 in long, April–June. The spike axis breaks apart at the nodes at maturity.
- **Awns:** long, sharp, can injure mouths, eyes, nasal passages, feet, and skin of grazing animals and pets ("foxtails" are this and similar species).
- **Seedbank:** seeds short-term dormant; most germinate the same fall. **Persistent seedbanks are unlikely** — soil longevity ~2 years (much shorter than oats or brooms).
- **Salt-tolerant**, can invade bare areas as native perennials die.

## Remove

Default method: [`mow_before_seed`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#mow_before_seed).

- **Small infestations:** dig or hand-pull. **Uproot before spike formation** — once awns harden, work becomes painful.
- **Mowing** somewhat suppresses but results are variable — plants typically recover if mowed before maturity. **Fewer seedheads** are produced when the plant regrows; mowing + biomass removal can reduce next-year germination.
- **Tillage** is effective on emerged plants but **stimulates subsequent emergence** — pair with herbicide follow-up.
- **Thick mulches** reduce germination.
- **Fire is unusually effective for this species** (WRIC notes a study where burning alone reduced cover from 90% to <5% for ~3 years). **Timing: ripe seed stage before seed disperses from the dead plants.**
- **Grazing** marginally effective with variable results — heavy grazing has reduced populations in some cases but moderate grazing can increase them.
- **Grass-selective herbicides** (clethodim, fluazifop) and non-selective (glyphosate) are options where chemical control is acceptable.

## Prevent

- **Pet safety:** the sharp awns ("foxtails") lodge in paws, ears, and noses — clip seedheads before they dry on properties with dogs.
- **Fire timing is the unusual lever for this species** — a well-timed burn at ripe seed stage can knock back hare barley for 3 years. Most other annual grasses do not respond this well to a single burn.
- **Establish competitive perennial vegetation** — hare barley invades bare ground as native perennials decline.
- **Clean equipment** — spikelets cling to fur, wool, and clothing.

## Why it's a problem

**Q1.1:** Like many other annual grasses, they could increase fire frequency. They typically do not get to densities high enough to do this, however.

**Q1.2:** Outcompetes perennial grasses, for example, by tolerating higher salinity and invading bare areas when perennials die. (1). But impacts in California are relatively minor (2).
