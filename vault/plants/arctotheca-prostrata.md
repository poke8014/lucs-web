# Arctotheca prostrata (capeweed)

Low rosette-forming groundcover in family Asteraceae, native to South Africa. Sterile capeweed (*A. prostrata*) is the form most commonly seen in California — fertile capeweed (*A. calendula*) is now considered a separate species with seed dispersal. Both names appear interchangeably in older literature.

In California: SF Bay area, north/central/south coast. Coastal prairies and disturbed areas; primarily a **coastal** invader. Not a strong threat in the South Bay foothills ([[regions/central-west]] inland portion).

## Identify

- Low rosette habit — typically under 6" tall, spreading wide via stolons
- Lobed gray-green leaves, woolly underside
- Yellow daisy-like flower heads (typical Asteraceae capitulum) in late winter/spring
- Forms dense monocultures that crowd out native ground-layer plants
- Often spotted along roadsides, in dumped landscape debris, and near former planting sites
- Photos pending — see [Cal-IPC profile](../raw/articles/calipc/arctotheca-prostrata-profile.md) for image references

## Remove (short-term)

> Cal-IPC has no published Weed RIC management notes for this species. The guidance below is inferred from the plant's biology as documented in the PAF; verify against UC Davis WRIC when those notes are ingested.

- **Hand-dig** the rosette and trace every stolon back to its rooting points. Capture all fragments.
- **Do not mow** — mowing fragments stolons and accelerates spread along roadsides (PAF Q2.3).
- **Do not compost** clippings. Stolons and tissue fragments will re-root in a home compost pile. Bag and dispose in green-waste landfill or burn.
- Tarp the work area to catch fragments before they touch soil.
- Best timing: before flowering (winter / early spring) to reduce any ornamental re-establishment temptation, though this plant's main threat is vegetative not seed-based.

## Prevent (long-term)

- **Watch your neighbors and the upstream right-of-way.** Capeweed travels in dumped landscape clippings — recurring infestations often trace back to a nearby planting or municipal mowing route.
- **Don't replace it with another groundcover invasive.** The bare patch left by removal is recolonized by whatever's most competitive. Native low groundcovers for coastal CA include yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*), beach strawberry (*Fragaria chiloensis*), and seaside daisy (*Erigeron glaucus*) — to be filled in as native plant pages are ingested.
- **Don't buy "sterile capeweed" as a sustainable replacement.** The "sterile" labelling addresses seed only; this is the form that spreads aggressively by stolons. See [[concepts/horticultural-introduction-pathway]].

## Why it's a problem

Forms dense monocultures (PAF Q1.1, Q1.2). A single small plant can cover 200 sq ft in 1-2 years; landscape plantings reach full cover within 6 months. Rapidly outcompetes native ground-layer plants for light, water, and space. Spread is overwhelmingly human-mediated — roadside mowing, dumped landscape debris, and intentional planting in wildlands are the main routes. See [[concepts/vegetative-spread]] for why this changes the management approach.
