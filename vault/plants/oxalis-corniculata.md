# Oxalis corniculata (creeping woodsorrel)

Creeping woodsorrel (*Oxalis corniculata*, family Oxalidaceae) is one of the most common residential weeds in California — a low, prostrate perennial with clover-like leaves and small yellow flowers that establishes in lawns, ornamental beds, groundcovers, and especially nursery and container stock. It reproduces both by a fleshy taproot with rooting stolons *and* by prolific, explosively-dispersed seed, which makes it far harder to eradicate than its delicate appearance suggests. *Oxalis stricta* (upright yellow woodsorrel) is a close congener managed the same way.

> [!info] Not to be confused with [[plants/oxalis-pes-caprae]]
> The UC IPM Pest Note "Creeping Woodsorrel and Bermuda Buttercup" covers two very different *Oxalis* weeds. **Bermuda buttercup** ([[plants/oxalis-pes-caprae]]) is a bulb-forming, **seedless** perennial controlled by digging the entire bulb chain. **Creeping woodsorrel** (this page) is a taprooted, **prolific-seeding** perennial — the management constraint is the seedbank and rooting stolons, not bulbs. Do not apply Bermuda buttercup advice to creeping woodsorrel.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7444 (updated 06/2010). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identify

- **Form:** prostrate (low, creeping) perennial that lives several seasons. Forms roots and stems wherever stem nodes contact the soil — it mats outward from a central fleshy taproot. Grows in both full sun and shade where there's adequate moisture.
- **Leaves:** 3 heart-shaped leaflets on a long stalk, green to purple; **fold downward in intense light and at night** (the field-ID trait shared by all *Oxalis*). Drought- or heat-stressed leaves turn reddish and wilt.
- **Flowers:** 5 small yellow petals, 1/8–1/3 in long, borne 2–5 per cluster on slender stalks. Can bloom almost any time of year; **spring is the heavy flowering and seed-set period**.
- **Seedpods (the management constraint):** erect, hairy, cylindrical capsules resembling miniature okra, 1/3–1 in long. Each holds 10–50 seeds; a single plant can produce **>5,000 seeds**. Mature pods **rupture and forcibly expel seed up to 10 ft**. Rough seeds stick to machinery, plastic pots, irrigation tubing, and clothing.
- **Germination:** seeds **require light** to germinate (so burial/mulch blocks them). Optimum 60–80 °F; can germinate any time of year in California, but **most establishment is in fall**. Seedlings flower in ~4 weeks. Soil seed-bank longevity is undocumented.
- **Root system:** a fleshy taproot plus an extensive outward-expanding root system. **Pulling snaps the taproot or stolons, leaving fragments that regrow** — pieces of roots and prostrate stems develop into new plants.

## Remove

Default method: [`dig_taproot`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_taproot).

- **Grub out established plants — don't just pull.** The taproot and rooting stolons break when yanked and regrow from fragments. Carefully handweed around desirable plants, removing as much stem and root as possible; several passes over a season are normal.
- **Mowing does not control it and spreads it.** Creeping woodsorrel sets seed even mowed as close as 1/4 in, and the more vigorous the turf the more vigorous the woodsorrel. **Wash or air-spray the mower** to remove seeds and clippings before mowing weed-free turf.
- **Block the seedbank with light:** because seeds need light, **2–3 in of organic mulch or landscape fabric** over cleared beds prevents germination. Use a mulch fine enough to fill gaps on the soil surface but **not so fine (e.g. sawdust) that it becomes a seedbed itself**. Replenish as it breaks down — any light reaching soil lets seeds germinate or fragments regrow.
- **Beds — preemergent + handweed (no selective postemergent exists):** dithiopyr, isoxaben, oryzalin, pendimethalin, or prodiamine prevent seedling emergence; apply in **early fall** (the germination peak), and two applications ~8 weeks apart for heavy infestations. These also inhibit ornamental seeds — read labels for bedding-plant safety. **No selective postemergent herbicide is available for woodsorrel once it has emerged in ornamental plantings.**
- **Turf — postemergent broadleaf herbicides:** **triclopyr** or **fluroxypyr** are highly effective on both seedlings and established plants; usually sold in 2-/3-/4-way broadleaf combinations specifically formulated for oxalis. **2,4-D alone is weak.** One triclopyr application often suffices (a follow-up 3–6 weeks later may be needed); most other products need a second application. **Triclopyr injures bermudagrass and kikuyugrass** — on warm-season lawns use fluroxypyr or a low-rate combination product instead. Preemergents (dithiopyr, isoxaben, pendimethalin, prodiamine) prevent re-establishment once existing plants are controlled; ~3 applications/year (early fall, midwinter, late spring) for heavy infestations.
- **Solarize before replanting:** clear plastic over moist soil for 4+ consecutive weeks in June–August reduces the seed population in heavily infested beds. See [`solarize_summer`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#solarize_summer).
- **Severely infested beds:** it can be faster to relandscape — salvage desirable plants, then solarize or repeatedly cultivate-and-irrigate to germinate and destroy seedlings before replanting.

## Prevent

- **Container/nursery stock is the major spread vector.** Inspect nursery pots and reject any with *Oxalis*; use potting soil free of woodsorrel seed; carefully remove any mature plants (all roots) before planting.
- **Don't move infested soil**, and clean tools, mowers, irrigation tubing, and clothing between infested and clean areas — rough seeds hitchhike.
- **Don't compost** trimmings containing woodsorrel — seed and stem fragments survive home compost.
- **Maintain dense competitive turf/groundcover and keep soil covered** — bare, lightly-mulched soil is exactly where new seedlings establish.
- **Plan for ongoing vigilance.** Seed-bank longevity is undocumented and new plants emerge from stem segments for several seasons; infested sites need continuous removal, with the heaviest pressure in fall.
