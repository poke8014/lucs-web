# Portulaca oleracea (common purslane)

Common purslane (*Portulaca oleracea*) is a succulent summer annual (family Portulacaceae) that forms dense prostrate mats in vegetable gardens, ornamental beds, low-maintenance lawns, and any bare warm-moist soil. First documented in the US in 1672 in Massachusetts. It's actually edible — a popular salad green in Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cooking, called *verdolaga* in Spanish — but as a garden weed it's a prolific seeder (a single plant can drop **240,000 seeds**) and the fleshy succulent stems re-root readily from cultivation, making one-pass mechanical control counterproductive.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7461 (updated 08/2007). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification and Life Cycle

- **Form:** prostrate, succulent annual forming a dense flat mat. Reddish stems radiate from a central rooting point like spokes of a wheel, commonly up to 12 in long.
- **Leaves:** stalkless, oval, smooth, shiny, succulent — 1/2 to 2 in long. Usually opposite but sometimes alternate near the base.
- **Flowers:** small (3/8 in), five-petaled, yellow. Borne singly in leaf axils; **open only in sunshine**.
- **Seeds:** reddish-brown to black, oval, tiny (1/64 to 1/32 in). Borne in a small pod with a top that comes off like a cookie-jar lid. Up to **240,000 seeds per plant**. Seeds remain viable **5–40 years** in soil.
- **Germination:** soil temperature ≥60°F triggers germination at or very near the soil surface, in large numbers after irrigation or rain. Late winter / early spring in southern desert areas; late spring in cooler areas.
- **Life cycle:** mature seedlings produce flowers within a few weeks; cycle continues all summer. Late-summer mats can be flipped to reveal thousands of seeds shed on the soil beneath.
- **The re-rooting trap:** fleshy stems **stay moist and viable for several days after cultivation**. Hoed plants that get re-irrigated will re-root from cut stem sections and form new plants — turning cultivation into transplanting.

## Remove

Default method: [`hand_pull`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#hand_pull) — but with one critical disposal rule.

- **Hand-pull while seedlings are small** — easiest after an irrigation when soil is moist.
- **Critical: don't leave pulled or hoed plants on damp soil.** Either remove them from the site, or let them thoroughly dry on hard surface (paving, tarp) before disposal. Stems re-root within days if dropped on moist ground. Plants pulled with seed already forming can continue ripening for a week after pulling — bag mature plants.
- **Cultivate / hoe shortly after each irrigation** to catch fresh seedlings — but understand cultivation can bring a fresh supply of seeds up from deeper soil for future germination.
- **Mulch (3+ in organic, or landscape fabric)** is the most reliable non-chemical control — purslane needs light to germinate, and a thick mulch starves it. Fabric mulches (porous) are preferred over plastic for irrigated beds.
- **Solarization** is one of the most effective non-chemical controls: cover moist soil with clear plastic for 4–6 weeks in July–August (when heat and light intensity are highest). Kills seeds in the top few inches. Don't cultivate afterward — that brings up deeper seeds. Prep beds before solarizing.
- **In turf:** healthy competitive grass usually outcompetes purslane. When it does establish, dicamba, MCPP, MSMA, 2,4-D are effective postemergents available to home gardeners. Preemergents (dithiopyr, pendimethalin, benefin combinations) work if applied before germination.
- **In ornamental beds:** mulch is usually sufficient. Spot glyphosate works on isolated plants — keep it off desirable foliage. Oryzalin, pendimethalin, trifluralin are home-gardener-available preemergents.
- **In vegetable gardens:** chemicals are almost never used — rely on solarization, mulch, and early cultivation of seedlings. Preemergent residues conflict with the diversity of vegetable plantings.
- **Purslane sawfly** is a known biocontrol but develops too slowly each season to matter for control — by the time it has impact, the seed crop is already set.

## Prevent

- **Prevention is the primary tool** — purslane is too prolific a seeder to easily knock back once established. A few scattered plants in year 1 → solid carpet in year 2.
- **Don't bring seed into clean areas.** Clean mowers, planters, cultivators between infested and clean beds. Use weed-free planting stock and seed.
- **Monitor uninfested sites after each irrigation or rain** and destroy seedlings before they set seed.
- **Edible:** purslane is a nutritious salad green (omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants). If you have a tolerated patch, pull it for the kitchen before flowering — denies the seed crop.
