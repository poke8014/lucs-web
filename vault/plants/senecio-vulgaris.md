# Senecio vulgaris (common groundsel)

Common groundsel (*Senecio vulgaris*) is a winter annual (family Asteraceae) found almost everywhere in California, especially in cool weather. It produces up to **25,000 seeds per plant** that wind-disperse readily, runs three or more generations per year, and starts seeding within ~6 weeks of germination — which makes it one of the fastest-spreading garden weeds in mild-climate residential beds. Seedbank is short (~1 year), so consistent removal before flowering substantially knocks back next year's population. **Pyrrolizidine alkaloids cause chronic liver poisoning in horses, cattle, and pigs** — even a small amount eaten over a few weeks; the hazard matters for properties with grazing animals.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 74130 (updated 05/2006). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identify

- **Form:** winter annual, up to **2 ft tall** in full sun. Often branched; shallow taproot + fibrous secondary roots. In shade, scraggly with fewer/smaller leaves.
- **Seedling first leaves:** shallow teeth, often purple on the underside, attached to the stem with a short petiole.
- **Later leaves:** more deeply lobed, attached directly to the stem. Alternate, mostly hairless.
- **Flower heads (key field ID):** clusters of small flowers surrounded by **green bracts with characteristic black tips** — separates from other yellow-flowered Asteraceae weeds.
- **Flowers:** bright yellow; ~6 weeks from sprouting to flowering. Mature into a **ball of white-tufted seeds** carried easily by wind.
- **Seed output:** up to 25,000 per plant under optimal conditions; ~1,700 more typical.
- **Seedbank:** ~1 year — short for a weed, which is the key management lever.
- **Hazard — pyrrolizidine alkaloids:** chronic liver-toxic to horses, cattle, swine. Critical to control near pasture areas.

## Remove

Default method: [`hand_pull`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#hand_pull).

- **Eliminate plants before they flower.** **Even pulled or cut plants can mature seeds from open flowers** — bag flowering plants and remove from the site rather than leaving on the ground.
- **Hand-pull, hoe, or cultivate shallowly** while plants are young. Start monitoring in early fall and continue through early summer.
- **Mulch (coarse, medium-size, 3+ in)** is highly effective — seedlings can't push through. Use a particle size large enough for water to pass through and the mulch surface to dry; fine mulches like sawdust absorb water and become a seedbed. Synthetic landscape fabrics block seeds already in the soil but **won't stop wind-blown seeds** from establishing on top.
- **Mowing** can help if the blade is set as close to the ground as possible; plants regrow from buds otherwise.
- **Chemical control** in home settings:
  - **Glyphosate or diquat** spot spray on small plants in landscape beds — shield the sprayer to avoid contact with desirable plants.
  - **Glyphosate is the only edible-crop-compatible option** of the two.
  - **No effective home-use preemergent** for this species.
- **Biocontrol:** the rust fungus *Puccinia lagenophorae* provides ~30% control in greenhouse tests — not yet commercially viable. No effective insect biocontrol.

## Prevent

- **One-year seedbank means the lever is high.** Two or three years of pulling plants before they flower substantially clears an infestation. The trap is that seeds can still mature on cut plants — remove them from the site.
- **Wind-dispersed seed travels easily** — a clean yard surrounded by infested neighborhood lots will re-infest from outside seed sources. Plan ongoing seedling removal.
- **Keep horses, cattle, and pigs away from infested areas** — small repeated doses cause chronic liver damage even when animals avoid the plant in normal forage.
- **Herbicide resistance:** this was one of the first weeds in California to develop resistance to common agricultural herbicides. If chemical control isn't working, mechanical removal is the more reliable path anyway.
