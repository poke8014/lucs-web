# Taraxacum officinale (dandelion)

Dandelion (*Taraxacum officinale*) is a perennial herb (family Asteraceae) that's the canonical lawn and landscape weed across most of California. Introduced from Europe, it's been used for centuries as an herb and medicinal plant — the leaves are edible and the roots make tea — but as an unmanaged invader of home lawns, ornamental beds, and turf it's hard to dislodge once established because of its deep taproot and prolific wind-dispersed seed.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7469 (updated 01/2018). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identify

- **Form:** perennial rosette plant with leaves clustered at a basal crown — **no true stems**, which is why mowing doesn't work (the crown sits below the blade).
- **Leaves:** 2–14 in long, 1/2–3 in wide, deeply serrated margins forming the "lion's tooth" silhouette the common name comes from (*dent-de-lion* in French).
- **Taproot (the management constraint):** strong, primarily in the top 6–18 in. Buds on the upper root produce a crown that regenerates new plants even when the visible plant is cut off at the soil surface. **Root sections as short as 1 in are capable of generating new plants.**
- **Flowering stalks:** 6–24 in tall, terminating in a single yellow inflorescence of 100–300 ray flowers. Flowers develop asexually (apomictic) — every plant is a seed source, no pollinator required.
- **Seeds:** wind-dispersed via the iconic puffball (pappus structures); seed can travel **miles** on the wind.
- **Bloom:** year-round in mild parts of California; dormant in cold intermountain winters.

## Remove

Default method: [`dig_taproot`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_taproot).

- **Solitary plants in beds or turf:** grub out the entire plant before it seeds — get all the taproot you can. A **dandelion knife** (or similar long, narrow weeding tool) lets you sever the taproot deep without disturbing too much soil.
- **Repeated hand-pulling and hoeing alone are usually futile** unless done diligently for years — buds on remaining root segments regenerate. The work is most successful in small home lawns and gardens where you can be persistent.
- **Mowing doesn't work** — the basal rosette sits below the mower blade. Mowing keeps the lawn neat without controlling dandelion.
- **Mulch (3+ in of wood chips / bark) or landscape fabric** suppresses germinating seedlings — light is required for germination, so light-blocking covers stop new establishment. The mulch has to be replenished as it breaks down.
- **Postemergent herbicides** (turf-safe): 2,4-D, dicamba, MCPA, mecoprop — usually sold as combination "weed-and-feed" products for lawns. Triclopyr is also effective but **don't use on Bermudagrass** or other warm-season turf. Carfentrazone and iron HEDTA give quick burndown on small seedlings.
- **Glyphosate** kills the plant but also any grass it contacts — only use on isolated plants where collateral damage is acceptable. Spot-paint glyphosate on leaves of plants in mulched beds.
- **Preemergents** (isoxaben, indaziflam, dithiopyr): apply to soil **before** seeds germinate; combine with hand-removal of existing plants to clean up the seedbank.
- **What doesn't work:** household 5% vinegar (not effective). Horticultural 20% vinegar can work but is a DANGER-signal-word product — eye protection and gloves required.

## Prevent

- **Catch new seedlings before they form a perennial taproot.** Once the crown develops (~8–15 weeks from germination), the work gets much harder.
- **Maintain dense competitive turf or ground cover.** Bare soil and stressed lawns are exactly where new dandelions establish.
- **Wind-dispersed seed travels miles** — eradicating dandelions in your yard doesn't prevent re-introduction from the neighborhood. Expect ongoing seedling sweeps every spring and fall.
- **Dandelion is edible**: leaves in salads, roots as tea. If you're keeping a small patch for foraging, sever flower stalks before seed set to limit downwind spread.
