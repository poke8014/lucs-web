# Cuscuta pentagona (field dodder) and related dodders

Dodder (*Cuscuta* spp.) is a **parasitic annual plant** — leafless, rootless once attached, threadlike yellow-to-orange stems that twine around host plants and tap into their vascular tissue with **haustoria** (specialized parasitic structures). 150+ species worldwide. **Field dodder (*C. pentagona*, also called *C. campestris*)** is the most common in California gardens — attacks tomatoes, peppers, melons, sugarbeet, asparagus, and many ornamentals plus weeds like field bindweed. **Japanese dodder (*C. japonica*)** is a separate, more dangerous species under **state eradication program** in California — if you see it, **don't try to control it yourself**; call your county Agricultural Commissioner. This page covers the more common native and naturalized dodders; Japanese dodder is noted but handled differently. Dodder seeds remain viable in soil for **20+ years**.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7496 (updated 03/2010). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification

- **Form:** rootless, leafless parasitic annual. **Slender twining or threadlike stems**, pale green to **yellow or bright orange** — the bright stems against the green host foliage are the diagnostic feature.
- **Leaves:** absent or reduced to **tiny scale-like triangular structures ~1/16 in long**.
- **Flowers:** **bell-shaped, cream colored, ~1/8 in long, in clusters** or singly. Each flower produces a seed capsule with 2–3 seeds.
- **Seeds:** rough-coated, ~1/16 in diameter. Yellowish threadlike seedlings emerge rootless and leafless.
- **Japanese dodder (*C. japonica*) — different management:**
  - **Stems thicker than other dodders — resemble spaghetti**, yellow-gold or green.
  - **Rarely flowers** in California — small pale yellow flowers when it does.
  - Spread by **stem fragments** (birds, pruning, composting) — no viable seeds observed in California.
  - **Targets trees and shrubs** (especially citrus), unlike native dodder species that target herbaceous hosts.
  - **Grows up to 6 inches per day** — much faster than native dodders.
- **Common host plants (native dodders):**
  - **Vegetables:** asparagus, beets, carrots, eggplant, garlic, melons, onions, peppers, potatoes, sweet potatoes, tomatoes.
  - **Ornamentals:** chrysanthemum, English ivy, fennel, impatiens, marjoram, mint, morning glory, periwinkle, petunia, summer savory.
  - **Weeds it also parasitizes:** field bindweed (*Convolvulus arvensis*), lambsquarters, pigweed, Russian thistle.
- **Lifecycle:** native dodder seeds germinate at or near the soil surface when temperatures reach ~60°F. **Seedling has 5–10 days to find a host before it dies.** Once it contacts a host, the stem coils around the plant, sends **haustoria into the host's vascular tissue**, and severs its own connection to the soil. The plant then runs entirely on the host's photosynthate.
- **Seed viability:** ~5% germinate the year after production; **the rest can remain dormant 20+ years**. Dormancy broken by scarification (cultivation, fire, microbial breakdown).
- **Damage:** ranges from moderate growth reduction to complete host death. Most severe when dodder attaches at the seedling stage of the host. Multiple attachments can kill established hosts. Japanese dodder can cover and kill mature shrubs and small trees.

## Remove

Default method: [`hand_pull`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#hand_pull) for native dodders; **call county Ag Commissioner for Japanese dodder.**

- **Japanese dodder — special handling.** Under California eradication program. **Don't try to control it yourself** — call your county Agricultural Commissioner for identification and removal. Spread to a dozen+ counties (Alameda, Butte, Contra Costa, Fresno, Los Angeles, Merced, Sacramento, Shasta, Solano, Sutter, Tulare, Yolo, Yuba).
- **Native dodder — catch seedlings before attachment.** Hand-pull or cultivate rootless yellow seedlings before they make haustoria. Once attached, the haustoria stay embedded in the host plant even if you pull the dodder off.
- **Once attached:** **prune the host plant 1/8–1/4 inch below the dodder attachment point.** If you only pull off the dodder stem, the embedded haustoria regenerate new dodder.
- **For extensive infestations:** remove and replace infested host plants with **non-hosts** — grasses and other monocots (lilies, irises, ornamental grasses), or transplanted trees and shrubs whose bark dodder can't penetrate. Rotate vegetable beds to non-host crops for several years.
- **Don't let pulled dodder make contact with new hosts** — freshly removed dodder can form new attachments if dropped on a healthy host plant. Bag and dispose in trash.
- **Mowing / cutting host plants** below the dodder attachment point reduces infestation. Close mowing works well in alfalfa.
- **Solarization** can kill seedlings but **dodder seeds survive solarization** because of the hard seed coat.
- **Composting may kill more dodder seed than solarization** because compost piles reach higher temperatures.
- **Burning:** must destroy the dodder-attached host tissue too, or haustoria regenerate. Burns kill only some seeds.
- **Preemergent herbicides** (trifluralin) before dodder seed germinates, in chronic problem areas.
- **Postemergent herbicides** are rarely useful: kill the dodder but injure the host plant. **Pelargonic acid (Scythe)** kills dodder but also any host tissue it contacts; spray carefully.
- **No effective resistant variety** breeding, though some tomato varieties show partial tolerance.

## Prevent

- **Plant dodder-free seed.** Most US states have seed laws prohibiting dodder contamination.
- **Inspect new transplants** — dodder fragments arrive in nursery plants. Bag and dispose if you find any.
- **Clean clothing, tools, and equipment** moving from infested to clean areas. Seeds spread in mud on shoes and tires.
- **Manage host-weed populations** — field bindweed and Russian thistle are dodder hosts, so they feed dodder populations even when crops are dodder-free.
- **20+ year seedbank** in chronically infested sites means long-term commitment to control.
