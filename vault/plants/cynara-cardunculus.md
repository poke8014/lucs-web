# Cynara cardunculus (artichoke thistle)

Cynara cardunculus(artichoke thistle) is a large perennial thistle (family Asteraceae) from the Mediterranean region found below 500 m elevations throughout California, except in the Great Basin and Desert Regions. Artichoke thistle prefers disturbed open sites, including grassland, chaparral, coastal scrub, and riparian areas. This thistle is closely related to cultivated artichokes ( Cynara scolymus), and the two species hybridize frequently. Artichoke thistle is also sometimes grown as an ornamental plant, and is available commercially. It reproduces by seed and sometimes by resprouting from root fragments. Rosettes can reach a diameter of well over one meter; leaves are large, spiny, and sharply lobed, and plants typically flower in May and June. When attempting control by mechanical removal, most of the plant’s large taproot must be removed to avoid resprouting.

## Identify

- **Form:** large **perennial** to 8 ft tall — the only true perennial in this group of taproot thistles.
- **Origin:** probably a wild biotype of commercial globe artichoke; **cultivated artichoke is the same species**.
- **Leaves:** **grayish, spiny, deeply pinnate-lobed** rosette leaves up to 3 ft long.
- **Taproot:** **deep fleshy taproot** developed in the first year — the persistence anchor for control.
- **Stems:** one or more erect, thick, branched stems with spiny ribs, topped with large purple flowerheads.
- **Flowers:** large solitary purple flowerheads, **1–6 in diameter** — look like oversized commercial artichokes.
- **Bloom:** spring through mid-summer (typically second year of growth, occasionally first year).
- **Reproduction:** primarily seed (most fall near the parent or wind-disperse up to 60 ft); **occasionally from root fragments after mechanical disturbance**.
- **Seedbank:** ~5 years in soil.
- **Lifecycle:** rosette leaves often die in summer, regrow in fall when rains start. Stems die after flowering but standing skeletons can persist >1 year.

## Remove

Default method: [`dig_taproot`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_taproot).

- **Seedlings:** cultivate or hand-pull. Easy at this stage.
- **Established plants:** **a large portion of the taproot must be removed**, or the remaining root regenerates. Use a mattock, post-hole digger, or shovel — labor is significant because of the depth.
- **Cut flower stems before maturity** to reduce seed production — even if you can't dig the whole taproot, denying seed input shortens the eventual cleanup.
- **Power tool / tractor cut** of dense patches at low height, followed by **herbicide treatment of regrowth**, is documented as effective for big infestations.
- **Foliar herbicides** (per WRIC):
  - Aminopyralid (Milestone) 5–7 oz/acre, postemergence in winter to spring before bolting.
  - Clopyralid (Transline) 0.25–0.67 pt/acre at seedling-to-rosette stage.
  - Triclopyr 0.25–1 gal/acre (Garlon 4 Ultra) up to bud stage.
  - 2,4-D 1.1–2.1 qt/acre at rosette.
- **Burning** removes aboveground dry skeletons in late summer/fall, facilitating subsequent herbicide treatment — but **burning alone does not kill the perennial taproot.**

## Prevent

- **Spreads with mechanical disturbance** — tillage and bulldozing can spread root fragments and create new plants. **One-pass disturbance is worse than no disturbance.**
- **Livestock avoid the spiny foliage**, but goats can graze rosettes and reduce seed production.
- **Coastal, overgrazed areas** are the typical risk profile — establish dense competitive vegetation to slow invasion.
- **Don't confuse cultivated artichoke escapes for ornamental cardoon** — they are the same species; both can establish viable infestations in the right conditions.

## Why it's a problem

**Q1.1:** Outcompetes native vegetation for light, water, and nutrients. No evidence of soil chemistry alteration because displaced species are able to recolonize following artichoke removal. Large arching leaves together with a large aggressive tap root system preemptively intercept resources necessary for the growth of other species. .

**Q1.2:** Artichoke thistle can create a monoculture leading to the the decline of, for example, broom baccharis (Baccharis sarothroides). Artichoke thistle is a threat to the endangered San Diego thornmint. Usually displaces annual exotic grasses, which may be facilitated by fire. Seriously threatens grassland ecosystems and may affect coastal sage scrub and riparian habitat in southern California. In San Diego's Los Penasquitos Canyon, artichoke thistle invades open forb covered canyon bottomlands. It can also invade riparian woodlands under willow (Salix spp.), mulefat (Baccharis glutinosa) and sycamore (Platanus racemosa). Artichoke thistle can reach stands of 22,000 plants per acre. Forms a basal rosette of leaves up to six feet in diameter. Reduces available habitat for grassland dependent species; displaces natives. There may be some alleopathic mechanism to neighbor plant suppression. When leaves die and fall to the ground they do not readily decompose, thus providing another barrier to competing species.
