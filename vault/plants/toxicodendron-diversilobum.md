# Toxicodendron diversilobum (Pacific poison-oak)

Pacific poison-oak (*Toxicodendron diversilobum*) is **native to California** and the western US — found from sea level to ~5,000 ft in coastlands, oak woodlands, rangelands, riparian areas, brushlands, and frequently in urban landscapes and parks. **This is the only native plant in the Sunshower cleanup-plan picker** — included **because of the urushiol allergen, not because of competition.** ~50–75% of California adults are allergic; the rash is the most common allergic contact dermatitis in North America. Forms a leafy shrub in sun (1–6 ft tall) or a climbing vine in shade (up to 75 ft). The classic ID rule "**leaves of three, let them be**" — three leaflets per leaf with the central leaflet on a longer stalk than the other two. Removal is a routine safety call near homes, paths, and play areas; otherwise it's a valuable native plant for wildlife (food source for deer, livestock, birds — none of which react to urushiol).

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7431 (updated 06/2021). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification

- **Form (varies by light):**
  - **Full sun:** dense leafy shrub, **1–6 ft tall.**
  - **Shaded forest/woodland:** climbing vine **up to 75 ft**, supports itself with aerial roots.
- **Leaves (the diagnostic feature):**
  - **3 leaflets per leaf**, the central leaflet on a **longer stalk** than the other two side leaflets.
  - **"Leaves of three, let them be."**
  - 1–4 in long per leaflet, **toothed or somewhat lobed margins**.
  - Surface ranges glossy to dull, thin to leathery, sometimes hairy on the underside.
  - **Alternate** along the stem (a true oak's leaves grow singly or in different arrangements).
  - Spring: green or light red.
  - Late spring/summer: glossy green.
  - **Fall:** **bright orange and red** — visually attractive but **more urushiol** in fall foliage, so worse exposure risk.
  - Winter: leafless (deciduous), but **stems still carry urushiol** if damaged.
- **Stems:** no thorns or spines (helps distinguish from blackberries). Climbing forms attach with aerial roots.
- **Flowers:** small, white-green, at leaf-attachment points.
- **Fruit:** **whitish-green, round, hairless** in late summer; persists into fall and winter. (Skunkbush sumac (*Rhus trilobata*) has hairy fruit and no extended-stalk on the central leaflet — non-toxic look-alike.)
- **Reproduction:** seed (hard coat, viable many years), suckering from rhizomes (new shoots in adjacent area), and **vine-tip rooting** where vines contact soil. Birds disperse seed widely — passage through bird digestion improves germination.

## The urushiol allergy

- **Urushiol** (yoo-ROO-shee-all) is the plant oil that causes allergic contact dermatitis.
- **50–75% of California adults are sensitive.** Lower in urban areas (~20%) because less childhood exposure.
- Peak sensitization 8–14 years of age.
- **Damage releases the oil** — slight contact with intact leaves is usually safe. Weed-eating, brush-clearing, pruning, trampling, mowing all break tissues and release urushiol.
- **Urushiol is non-volatile, persists on tools, clothing, pets, vehicles for months or years.** Cars and dogs are common re-exposure vectors.
- **Burning poison-oak is dangerous** — oil disperses in smoke and causes severe respiratory irritation.
- **Wash exposed skin within 10 minutes** to remove ~50% of the oil; 30 minutes too late (oil absorbed).
- **Removal technique:** **forceful unidirectional washing** with a damp washcloth and **liquid dishwashing soap** under warm running water — repetitive single-direction high-pressure wipes, ~3 minutes × 3 rounds. (Not violent back-and-forth scrubbing, which spreads the oil and irritates skin.) Wash hands and under fingernails thoroughly — fingernails are the main re-transfer route to the face.
- **Wash all exposed clothing, tools, and pets** in soap and water after work.
- **OTC products** like Tecnu, Zanfel, and various specialty soaps also remove urushiol effectively.

## Remove

Default method: [`pull_vine_dig_crown`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#pull_vine_dig_crown).

- **Safety first:** wear **washable cotton gloves over plastic gloves**, long sleeves, long pants, closed shoes. Wash all clothing and equipment afterward. **Have someone who is genuinely immune do the work if you have a sensitivity.**
- **Don't burn poison-oak.** Urushiol disperses in smoke; respiratory exposure can be severe.
- **Don't mow poison-oak.** Mowers release oil particles into the air, same exposure hazard as burning.
- **Hand-pull or mechanical-grub** when soil is moist (early spring or late fall). Remove **the entire plant including roots**. Dry hard soil → stems break off and rootstocks vigorously resprout.
- **Lopping branches near the base** without herbicide → vigorous resprouting. Combine with herbicide for effective control.
- **Repeated rototilling** through the year is effective in a garden bed. **Single-pass rototilling fragments rhizomes and spreads the plant.**
- **Foliar herbicides** (most home-gardener-available options):
  - **Glyphosate 2% v/v** spray-to-wet (2.5 oz of 41% concentrate per gallon): good to excellent control. Late spring / early summer.
  - **Triclopyr ester 1–5% v/v** spray-to-wet: very effective. Use with seed oil (Hasten-EA at 1%) for better penetration.
  - **Triclopyr amine 3–6% v/v**: also effective. Use at temperatures over 80°F (less vaporization than ester).
  - **Apply when actively growing** — late spring or early summer for most products. Air temp >80°F → use amine triclopyr or glyphosate, not ester triclopyr.
- **Cut-stump treatment:** cut, then within minutes paint the stump with concentrated triclopyr ester (1:4 with seed oil), undiluted triclopyr amine 8%, or glyphosate 41% concentrate diluted 1:1 in water.
- **Basal bark application** for woody stems: 20% triclopyr ester in 80% seed oil, applied to a 6–12 in band around the basal stem. Effective most of the year, even after leaves drop.
- **Re-treat when new shoots are about 2 ft tall.** Single applications rarely give complete control; monitor and re-spray for 1+ year.
- **Grazing:** sheep and goats graze poison-oak without reaction. Deer, horses, and cattle eat young foliage too. **Effective control in small areas.**

## Prevent

- **Identify poison-oak before doing any brush work** — "leaves of three, let them be" is the basic rule. Skunkbush sumac is the most common look-alike but has hairy fruit and equal-stalked leaflets.
- **Catch new seedlings in mulched beds and lawn edges** — birds drop seeds and new plants establish under tree canopies. Easier to pull seedlings than mature plants.
- **Maintain awareness in fall and winter** — winter stems are leafless but still carry urushiol. Don't assume "no leaves" means "no exposure."
- **Don't try to immunize via ingestion** — old folk methods (eating leaves) don't work and can cause severe internal reactions. A research vaccine (PDC-APB) is in clinical trials but not yet available.
- **Poison-oak is a valuable native species** in wildland settings — only remove it where it poses a safety or access problem (near homes, trails, play areas, pastures). Don't eradicate from natural habitat just because it can cause a rash.

## Note on inclusion

Poison-oak is the only native plant in the Sunshower cleanup-plan picker (per the [[feedback-sunshower-weed-scope]] direction). It's included because **homeowners routinely remove it from yards for safety reasons** — the cleanup-plan covers "plants you want gone," not just non-native invasives. The plant page carries `nativity: native` (not `invasive`) so structured queries can distinguish it from the rest.
