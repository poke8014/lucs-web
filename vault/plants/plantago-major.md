# Plantago major (broadleaf plantain) and buckhorn plantain

Broadleaf plantain (*Plantago major*) and buckhorn plantain (*Plantago lanceolata*) are the two main weed plantains in California — perennial rosette weeds in turf, ornamental beds, gardens, roadsides, and pastures. Both grow year-round except in the coldest intermountain areas. Like dandelion, they grow from a tight crown at the soil surface and **mowing has no effect** — leaves and seed stalks emerge below the blade. UC IPM treats both species under one Pest Note; this page does the same, with broadleaf plantain as the primary slug. **Broadleaf plantain is an indicator of compacted, alkaline, low-fertility soil**; buckhorn plantain is an indicator of dry, low-fertility soil — both signal underlying conditions worth fixing.

Source: UC IPM Pest Note PN 7478 (updated 02/2020). See [[sources/ucipm-residential]].

## Identification

- **Broadleaf plantain (*P. major*):** smooth oval leaf blades **2–7 in long**, with **several veins running parallel to the leaf margins** — distinctive parallel venation in a broadleaf plant. Veins meet at the base to form a broad petiole. Flowering stalks 15 in tall, topped by a dense spike. Short crown with fibrous roots.
- **Buckhorn plantain (*P. lanceolata*):** longer narrower leaves **3–12 in long**, parallel veins, smooth blade-to-petiole transition. Taproot (vs. fibrous roots of broadleaf). Crown covered with **tan woolly hairs**. Flowering stalks 18 in tall — when blooming, stamens protrude visibly from the flower head.
- **Seeds:** black or brown, ~1/16 in. Germinate at the soil surface when temperatures reach 50°F (optimal 77°F).
- **Habitat:**
  - **Broadleaf:** moist, full sun to partial shade, compacted soil. Frequently mowed turf.
  - **Buckhorn:** disturbed sites, drier conditions, less irrigation.
- **Crown system:** **both species grow from the crown at the soil surface** — like dandelion, this is why mowing fails to control. Plants regenerate from the crown even when cut off at or below soil level. Crowns of broadleaf plantain can grow **6–10 in across** with thickened fibrous roots; buckhorn has a stout taproot.

## Remove

Default method: [`dig_taproot`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_taproot) for buckhorn; persistent crown removal for broadleaf.

- **Remove seedlings before they produce seed.** Mature plants are hard to dislodge because of the persistent crown.
- **Hand-pull / dig / hoe** mature plants — **must remove the entire crown** (and taproot for buckhorn) or it regenerates. Repeat over multiple months in a season.
- **Mowing doesn't control plantain** — the rosette and seed stalks both stay too low to be cut by a mower blade.
- **Mulch (2–4 in organic, or landscape fabric + cover mulch)** controls seedlings. Reapply organic mulch as it degrades.
- **In turf:** maintain a dense healthy sward. Address the underlying compaction (for broadleaf) or low irrigation (for buckhorn). Dig out mature plants repeatedly for several years to fully eradicate.
- **Postemergent turf herbicides** (best applied in fall):
  - **2,4-D** works best for mature plants in turf — repeat applications often needed.
  - **Triclopyr, dicamba, carfentrazone, mesotrione, MCPA, mecoprop** — some control mature plants, others only suppress.
  - **Quinclorac:** fair control on buckhorn, poor on broadleaf.
- **Preemergent options in turf:** indaziflam, isoxaben, mesotrione (home-gardener available); atrazine and flumioxazin licensed-only.
- **In ornamental beds:** few effective options — hand-pull, mulch, and spot glyphosate (with care). **Nonselective contact herbicides** (diquat, glufosinate, pelargonic acid, acetic acid, fatty acids) kill seedlings but only top-kill mature plants.

## Prevent

- **Plantain's persistent crown** is the main management constraint. Removing seedlings early — within their 8–15 week seedling stage — is far easier than digging mature plants.
- **Fix the underlying soil condition.** Broadleaf plantain → compaction + alkalinity + low N; buckhorn → dry + low fertility. Address the soil, and the lawn outcompetes plantain.
- **Clean equipment** to avoid spreading seed and vegetative fragments.
- **Plantain is edible** (young leaves used in traditional medicine) — if you're maintaining a small foraging patch, cut flowering stalks before seed set.
