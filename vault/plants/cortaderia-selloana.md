# Cortaderia selloana (pampasgrass)

Cortaderia selloana (pampasgrass) is a large perennial grass (family Poaceae) found along the coast of California, and in the Coast Ranges, Central Valley, Western Transverse Ranges, and Mojave Desert. Pampasgrass favors dunes, bluffs, coastal shrublands and marshes, inland riparian areas, and disturbed areas. It was introduced as an ornamental plant and for erosion control. Each plume produces up to 100,000 seeds that are widely dispersed by wind and develop without fertilization. Pampasgrass quickly colonizes bare ground, but establishment is generally poor where the seedlings must compete with other grasses or sedges. It is native to Argentina, Brazil and Uruguay, where it grows in relatively damp soils along river margins.

## Identify

- **Form:** large, densely tufted perennial grass with **long basal leaves** and tall plume-like inflorescences. Larger tussocks than [[plants/cortaderia-jubata]] — characteristically **fountain-shaped**.
- **Plumes:** 1–3 ft long, **silvery cream-to-white** (vs the pinkish-purple of *C. jubata*). Male plumes can be purplish-tinted.
- **Leaves (HAZARD):** sharply serrated margins — same skin-cutting risk as jubatagrass. Heavy leather gloves and long sleeves required.
- **Roots:** dense fibrous roots from shallow short lateral rhizomes.
- **Reproduction (key difference from jubata):** **plants are functionally male or female** (dioecious). Seed develops **only when male and female plants are within pollination range** of one another. Historically, female plants were exclusively propagated in the nursery trade for ornamental plumes; recent seed-grown nursery stock includes both sexes, which is why weedy populations spread quickly near recent ornamental plantings.
- **Seed output:** up to 100,000 seeds per fertilized female plume, wind-dispersed up to ~20 miles.
- **Seedbank:** seeds survive <6 months in field — no persistent seedbank.
- **Climate tolerance:** wider than jubatagrass (more variable winters, drier interior sites). Found in coastal CA and inland riparian / interior sites.

## Remove

Default method: [`dig_rhizome_complete`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_rhizome_complete). Identical playbook to jubatagrass.

- **Hand-pull seedlings.**
- **Established clumps:** Pulaski, mattock, or shovel to remove the **entire crown and top section of roots**. Detached plants can re-root on moist soil — turn upside-down to dry, or remove from site.
- **Chainsaw or weed-eater** exposes the base for safer crown removal.
- **Cut plumes before seed dispersal** as a stopgap; expect more plumes during the flowering season.
- **Foliar glyphosate** is the most consistent treatment (per WRIC, same recipe as jubatagrass):
  - Broadcast 2–3.3 qt/acre.
  - 2% v/v spray-to-wet spot.
  - **8% v/v low-volume** treatment gave the best and most consistent control in trials.
  - **Wiper applicator at 33–50% concentrate** on every tiller — high selectivity for desirable plants nearby (the recommended technique per [synthesis page](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#dig_rhizome_complete)).
- **Timing:** late summer or fall, after flowering, when herbicide translocation to rhizomes is at peak.
- **Imazapyr** at 2–4% v/v is a slower-acting alternative; can take 1–2 years for effective control.
- **No biocontrol**, **no effective grazing**, **no effective burning** in CA conditions.

## Prevent

- **Avoid soil disturbance / bare ground** — invasion follows.
- **Female-only ornamental plantings** were the traditional path; in older landscapes, an isolated female *C. selloana* clump may have been quiet for decades — but **once a male plant arrives nearby, seed production explodes** and the site rapidly becomes an outbreak source.
- **Newer seed-grown nursery stock** carries both sexes — voluntary nursery avoidance (PlantRight, etc.) is the primary prevention.
- **Watch for wind-blown seedlings** downwind of known fertile patches.

## Why it's a problem

**Q1.1:** fire intensity, fire frequency These large plants produce significant amounts of biomass that is extremely flammable, increasing both the frequency and intensity of fire. The large size also significantly reduces light availability, especailly in mono-specific stands.

**Q1.2:** Alters plant community conposition and structure. Develops mono-specific stands with >75% cover, eliminates lower layers, displaces native species, and creates a new layer in maritime chaparral and other scrub ecossystems. San Diego plants?
