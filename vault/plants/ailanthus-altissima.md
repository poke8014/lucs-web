# Ailanthus altissima (tree-of-heaven)

Ailanthus altissima (tree-of-heaven) is a deciduous tree (family Simaroubaceae) that is widely, but somewhat discontinuously distributed in California. It is native to parts of China and Taiwan and first introduced as a landscape ornamental to Europe and the United States in the 1700’s. Since then, it has escaped gardens, established in the wild, and spread widely through seeds and creeping roots that produce many suckers. In California, it is most abundant along the coast and mountain foothills, primarily in wastelands and disturbed, semi-natural habitats. Tree-of-heaven is considered invasive because of its ability to establish, spread quickly and dominate landscapes, as well as its allelopathic properties, which help to suppress surrounding vegetation. It can be distinguished by its large, pinnately compound leaves with often asymmetrical lanceolate leaflets. Leaves can smell like rancid peanut butter when crushed (unlike similar-looking walnut trees). Stems will show large heart-shaped leaf scars. Fruits are samaras. More recently, concern about tree-of-heaven has increased because it is a preferred host for a destructive and invasive insect pest, the [spotted lanternfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotted_lanternfly).

## Identify

- **Form:** fast-growing deciduous tree, up to nearly 70 ft tall.
- **Leaves (key field ID):** very large pinnate-compound leaves (1–3 ft long), 11–41 leaflets per leaf. **Crushed leaves and twigs have an unpleasant skunky / peanut-butter odor.**
- **Bark:** smooth, pale gray-brown, with light-colored vertical streaks on young trunks.
- **Roots:** **long, deep taproot with many creeping roots that sucker freely** — creeping roots may extend ~50 ft in all directions. **New shoots can sprout up to 50 ft from the parent tree**, often growing >3 ft per year.
- **Flowers / fruit:** dioecious; female trees produce winged samaras in clusters. One tree can produce **>325,000 seeds annually**, most viable. Wind-dispersed; most fall near the parent.
- **Seedbank:** seeds survive **only ~1 year** in soil — short seedbank, but constant annual recruitment makes that mostly academic.
- **Lifespan:** typically 30–50 years.
- **Hazards:** brittle wood (tree-fall risk near buildings), root damage to pavement and foundations, allelopathic compounds suppress nearby plants, possible contact dermatitis from foliage in sensitive people, pollen allergen.
- **Distinguish from native sumacs:** Ailanthus leaflets have a few large coarse teeth near the base (with glands underneath); native sumacs (*Rhus* spp.) have toothed leaflets along the entire margin.

## Remove

Default method: [`cut_stump_herbicide`](../synthesis/invasive-removal-methods.md#cut_stump_herbicide).

- **Hand-pull seedlings only** — once creeping roots have developed, hand removal generally fails.
- **Saplings or small trees:** weed wrench / woody-weed extractor when soil is moist. **Extract the entire root or stump-sprouting will occur**, plus suckering from the spreading root system.
- **Cutting alone produces a thicket of root suckers** — far worse than the original tree. Never cut without chemical follow-up.
- **Most effective treatment (per WRIC): stem injection ("drill and fill")** with **imazapyr** (Arsenal / Habitat, undiluted, 1 ml per hack, one hack per 3 inches of stem diameter). Imazapyr has the most consistent results across studies. Apply mid-June to mid-September (before fall color).
- **Cut-stump treatment** (within minutes of cutting):
  - Triclopyr ester (Garlon 4 Ultra) 20–25% in oil carrier.
  - Glyphosate 41% concentrate diluted or undiluted.
  - Imazapyr 20% in 80% water (Arsenal/Habitat) or oil carrier (Stalker/Chopper).
- **Basal bark treatment** as alternative for small-to-medium trees: 20–25% Garlon 4 Ultra in oil carrier sprayed on the lower 12–15 in of trunk. Don't cut for 4 months after.
- **Foliar spray** for saplings only: triclopyr 1–2% or glyphosate 2–4% + surfactant.
- **No biocontrol** agents available.
- **Heavy shade reduces establishment** — useful for prevention but not removal.

## Prevent

- **Never cut Ailanthus without a planned herbicide follow-up** within minutes. This is the single biggest mistake homeowners make with this species.
- **Track suckers for 1–2 years** after main tree removal — new shoots can appear 50 ft from the felled tree.
- **Don't move soil from infested sites** — root fragments can establish elsewhere.
- **Tree-of-heaven is a stop-spread priority** in CA — a single mature female near a watercourse seeds an entire downstream corridor.

## Why it's a problem

**Q1.1:** Score C: Changes soil chemistry through allelopathy (ailanthone) Toxic levels maintained through the growing season although Heisey, R.M. states soil microbes rapidly detoxify ailanthone.

**Q1.2:** A; Displaces native plants. Produces many seeds, forms taproot readily, forms abundant root sprouts, clonal ramets. Can form monospecific stands, eliminating other vegetation. Combined with toxins, these strategies appear to give it a competitive advantage. Information about response to shade is contradictory. Per Grime (1965) Ailanthus has a high degree of shade tolerance per Grime 1965 (as cited by Hunter) but Kowarik, 1995, characterizes Ailanthus as a pioneering light-demanding species that may use clonal ramets to overcome lack of light.
