# Planting in a Post-Wild World

**Thomas Rainer & Claudia West**, *Planting in a Post-Wild World: Designing Plant Communities for Resilient Landscapes*. Timber Press, Portland, Oregon (2015).

A design-theory manifesto for **[[concepts/designed-plant-communities|designed plant communities]]** — arranging plants the way they grow in the wild (interlocking, vertically layered, ground fully covered) rather than as isolated ornamental objects spaced apart in mulch. This is the most important design-theory source in the vault so far: it is the theoretical backbone for Sunshower's density-style and per-section direction, and it supplies the vocabulary for the planting-layer and [[concepts/plant-sociability|sociability]] models. See [[synthesis/post-wild-world-and-sunshower]] for how the book maps onto the app roadmap.

## Who wrote it, and the stance

Rainer (American landscape architect) and West (German-born horticulturist/plantswoman) write from two vantage points on nature — "nature lost" (American memory of vanishing wilderness) and "nature regained" (post-industrial European landscapes healing). Their shared argument: the front line for nature is not remote wilderness but **our own yards, medians, and parking lots**, and the way to bring nature back there is to design *plantings that function like wild communities* — resilient, layered, low-input — without pretending to be ecological restoration.

Two framing moves matter for how we read the book against Sunshower's [[concepts/right-plant-right-place|RPRP thesis]]:

- **It is deliberately not native-purist.** The authors elevate *plant-to-place fitness and performance* over country-of-origin, and explicitly argue adapted exotics can belong in a community (the exception being anything with invasive potential). This is a **friendly contradiction** with Sunshower's native-first thesis — we take the book's *methods* (how to plant) while keeping our *palette* rule (native CA plants). Flagged on [[concepts/right-plant-right-place]] and in the synthesis page; not silently adopted.
- **Its plant examples are Eastern-US and European**, not Californian (Panicum, Eutrochium, Baptisia, Carex pensylvanica, Mediterranean sages…). The *principles* transfer to California; the *plant lists* do not. Per RPRP regional scoping, **no plant pages were created from this source** — the same call made for the Zone-7a reddit thread.

## The five principles (Ch. 1)

The book's core, distilled — each is developed on [[concepts/designed-plant-communities]]:

1. **Related populations, not isolated individuals** — plants are groups of compatible species interacting with each other and the site, not furniture placed for decoration.
2. **Stress as an asset** — accept a site's constraints (poor soil, shade, drought) instead of amending them away; stress is what produces a strong sense of place and self-limiting, well-behaved plants.
3. **Cover the ground densely by vertically layering plants** — the single most important idea; bare soil is an unfilled niche that weeds will take. "Green mulch" (plants) replaces bark mulch.
4. **Make it attractive and legible** — translate messy ecological function into ordered, culturally acceptable form (see [[concepts/orderly-frames]]).
5. **Management, not maintenance** — manage the whole community toward design goals with occasional large-scale actions (mow, edit, add), rather than propping up individuals with water/fertilizer/pruning on a fixed schedule (see [[concepts/management-not-maintenance]]).

## What's novel here (vs. prior vault sources)

- A **whole-community** model of planting, where the unit of design is the interlocking mix, not the specimen. Prior vault sources (Pretty Purple Door, UC IPM) are individual-plant and site-condition oriented.
- The **four-layer system** with target proportions — structural, seasonal theme, ground cover, filler — split into "design layers" (legible) and "functional layers" (diverse). See [[concepts/planting-layers]].
- **Levels of sociability (1–5)** as a design axis — the exact rating Sunshower's backlog was waiting on. See [[concepts/plant-sociability]].
- Three **plant-strategy systems** (Grime's C-S-R, Kühn's 8 types, Hansen's habitat method) as selection tools. See [[concepts/plant-strategies-csr]].
- **Landscape archetypes** (grassland / woodland / forest / edge) + a dichotomous "landscape selection key" for reading a site's goal community. See [[concepts/landscape-archetypes]].
- A **management lifecycle** — three establishment phases (plant establishment → landscape establishment → post-establishment), each with different goals and monitoring. See [[concepts/management-not-maintenance]].

## What overlaps / corroborates prior pages

- **Density beats spacing.** The book's spacing chapter directly contradicts the container-radius spacing math on [[concepts/plant-spacing]] — spacing is per-*layer* by mature size, and density comes from vertical layering, not tight single-layer packing. Flagged there.
- **Site reading.** "Exploration before analysis," fieldwork, sketching, and archetype reading enrich [[concepts/site-inventory]].
- **Stress-as-asset ≈ amend-less-choose-better.** Strongly corroborates the [[concepts/soil-basics]] / RPRP guidance already in the vault, from a design-theory angle.
- **Ground-cover density as weed suppression** dovetails with the cleanup-plan's [[synthesis/invasive-removal-methods|reinfestation-prevention]] logic: a filled niche is the durable defense against weeds.

## Structure (for citation)
