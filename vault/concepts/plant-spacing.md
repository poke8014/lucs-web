# Plant Spacing

How far apart to plant individual plants and how far they should sit from buildings, paths, and property lines — measured against the plant's **mature size**, not its size at the nursery.

The most common beginner mistake is planting too close to the foundation, too close to neighboring plants, or both. A 1-gallon shrub from the nursery looks tiny; the same plant five years later may be 8' wide. Planning for the mature size up front avoids costly transplant work — or worse, foundation damage from oversized shrubs against the house.

## The math

Plant tags list mature **height × width**. Width is what matters for spacing; the *radius* (half the width) determines how far the trunk should sit from any neighbor or structure.

For a shrub rated **4'H × 5'W**:
- Mature radius: 2.5' from center.
- A neighboring 5'W shrub planted trunk-to-trunk needs at least 5' (the two radii sum) for foliage to just touch at maturity.
- For breathing room (no overcrowding), space **6' trunk-to-trunk** so foliage doesn't touch.

## Foundation planting

Distances from the house wall need extra room:

- **Minimum:** ≥ 1' of open space between mature foliage and the foundation. For a 5'W shrub that's a trunk-to-wall distance of **3.5'**.
- **Ideal:** ≥ 2' open space. Trunk-to-wall **4.5'**.

Why the extra clearance:
- Window light and airflow at the foundation.
- Roots near the foundation can affect drainage and (with very large shrubs/trees) the foundation itself.
- Maintenance access — you need to reach the wall to paint, repair, prune.

## When you've already planted too close

The source advises moving young plants while they're still small and the weather is cool. Most perennials and small shrubs transplant well in early spring or fall. Larger shrubs and trees become much harder to move; for those, plan correctly the first time.

## Considerations the source doesn't address

- **Drift planting and visual massing.** For pollinator gardens specifically, planting in **drifts of the same species** (3+ plants of one kind clustered together) is often recommended to make foraging efficient for bees. Strict trunk-to-trunk spacing rules conflict with the visual goal of a tight drift. Pollinator-design sources need to weigh in on how to reconcile this.
- **Native plants and competition.** Native communities are denser than ornamental beds and accept more proximity — chaparral, for instance, is a tight matrix of overlapping shrubs. The "give everyone breathing room" rule is an ornamental-garden norm that may not transfer to a habitat-oriented planting.
- **Groundcovers.** Spacing is a different game for spreading groundcovers (rate of spread per year matters as much as final extent). Worth its own treatment.

## Relationship to other concepts

- One step in the [[concepts/bubble-drawing]] / layout-iteration process — once features and beds are placed, plant spacing fills in the bed.
- Constrains plant selection: a 6'W shrub is impossible in a 4'-wide bed against the house no matter how much you like it.
