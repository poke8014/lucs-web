# Watering

How much, how often, and (importantly) for how long after planting. The default beginner failure is *under*-watering during establishment and *over*-watering after.

## The 1-inch-per-week rule of thumb

Most established perennials and shrubs in temperate climates need roughly **1 inch of water per week**, *combined* from rain and irrigation. A simple **rain gauge** in the garden tells you how much rain fell that week — anything below 1" is the gardener's responsibility.

Conversion to volume:
- **1 inch of water = ~0.6 gallons per square foot** of bed area.
- For a 50 sq ft bed: 1" ≈ 30 gallons/week.

Practical timing trick: fill a 5-gallon bucket with your watering tool (hose, can, or drip line on a timer) and time how long it takes. Then you know the duration that delivers a known volume — no guesswork.

## Technique: water the rootball, not the leaves

Beyond volume, *how* you deliver the water matters at least as much. Two operational rules from [[sources/gardenersworld-beginner-tips]]:

- **Water the rootball, not the leaves.** Roots absorb water; foliage doesn't. Wetting foliage wastes water and can encourage some fungal diseases.
- **One deep soak per week beats a shallow daily sprinkle.** Frequent shallow watering encourages plants to root near the surface, which makes them *less* drought-tolerant and worse at finding their own water. A weekly deep soak trains roots downward.

These two rules apply broadly. The "deep, infrequent" rule applies *especially* hard to mature drought-tolerant plants and to most established California natives — the opposite watering pattern from what the rule of thumb otherwise implies.

## Feeding (briefly)

[[sources/gardenersworld-beginner-tips]] recommends feeding regularly during the growing season (spring/summer), roughly fortnightly, with extra feeding for plants in containers (which have a limited nutrient reservoir).

This is **generic UK-context advice that should not be applied blindly to California natives.** Many CA natives evolved on lean soils and can be *harmed* by feeding — fertilizer encourages weak, soft growth that's more vulnerable to pests, disease, and frost. A CA-native-specific feeding page is overdue and should be written when a CA-native source covers the topic.

## Establishment period

Newly planted plants need more frequent watering until their roots reach into surrounding soil:

- **Perennials and small shrubs:** water regularly for the first few weeks (when the topsoil dries to ~1" depth).
- **Trees and large shrubs:** consistent watering for the **first year**, sometimes two. Even drought-tolerant species need establishment watering.

Once established, drought-adapted plants should be weaned off supplemental water.

## Caveats — and why this rule of thumb may not transfer to native CA gardens

The 1"/week rule comes from generic Eastern/temperate gardening literature. It applies poorly to several California native scenarios:

- **California natives evolved with seasonal drought.** Many of them — California poppy, sages, manzanitas, ceanothus — *die* from summer water once established. They expect dry summers and wet winters. The "1 inch a week year-round" rule kills them.
- **Coastal vs. inland matters.** Coastal foggy zones provide significant moisture to plants from fog drip even with no rain. Inland summer watering needs are very different.
- **Soil texture matters.** Sandy soils need more frequent, smaller watering; clay soils need less frequent, deeper watering.
- **Mature drought-tolerant plants want deep, infrequent water.** A shallow daily sprinkle promotes shallow rooting and worse drought tolerance — opposite of what the gardener wants.

This page deliberately keeps the 1"/week rule with explicit caveats. A native-CA-specific watering page (likely covering establishment watering for natives, summer dry-down, and irrigation zones) is overdue and should be created when a CA-native source is ingested.

## Tools mentioned

- **Rain gauge** — cheap, the simplest way to know how much rain you got.
- **Drip irrigation** — vastly more efficient than overhead spray for a planted bed; deserves its own concept page eventually.
