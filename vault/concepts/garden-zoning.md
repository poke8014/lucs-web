# Garden Zoning

**Garden zoning** is the practice of dividing a yard into named functional areas — *rooms* — before any planting decision. Each zone has a use (cooking, sitting, growing, playing, tool storage, wildlife), a set of conditions (sun, shelter, proximity to the house), and a layout that follows from both.

Zoning slots between the [[concepts/site-inventory]] step (where conditions are mapped) and the planting step (where plants are placed). The conditions captured in the inventory tell you *which* zone goes *where*; the zone definitions tell you what design and plant choices each zone calls for.

## Why it matters

Counter-intuitive observation from [[sources/littleterracedhouse-designing-a-garden]]: a yard divided into zones can *feel bigger* than the same yard treated as one undifferentiated space. The author's small (~6m × 16m) terrace garden gained perceived space once it was organized into a potting area, a family dining patio, a lawn/borders zone, a sunny patio, and a veg patch.

Practically, zoning also forces alignment between use and microclimate. A "family dining area in the coldest, shadiest corner" doesn't pencil out, and you only notice that by naming the zones explicitly. The bottom-patio-as-potting-station decision in the source is exactly this match: too cold to sit on, but morning sun is plenty for a grow house.

## Common zones

There's no canonical list — the right zones depend on the household. Common candidates:

- **Cooking / dining** — outdoor table, BBQ, possibly outdoor kitchen. Wants shelter from wind, late-afternoon sun, easy reach to the kitchen.
- **Sitting / lounging** — arbor, bistro set, hammock. Wants warmth and a view.
- **Growing — ornamental** — borders, beds, pollinator plantings. Wants sun matched to plant choices and reasonable access for maintenance.
- **Growing — edible** — veg patch, herb garden. Wants full sun, close to a water source.
- **Play** — for kids; for pets. Wants soft ground, line-of-sight from inside the house.
- **Utility / potting** — composting, potting bench, tool shed, hose hub. Can occupy the *least* desirable zone (shaded, cold corner, behind a structure).
- **Wildlife** — pond, log pile, brush stack, wildflower meadow patch, bird feeders. Often the zone where things look "messier" by suburban standards.

## Sequence: site inventory → zone the yard → place plants

The author's hard-won lesson is to **work in logical order**:

1. Clear unwanted features (plastic grass, old patio, decking).
2. Position lighting (call the electrician early).
3. Replace fences if needed (do this *before* planting climbers).
4. Landscape (patios, walls, decking, pergolas).
5. Add furniture and large items (sheds, dining tables).
6. Shape borders.
7. **Plant last.**

The regret-narrative driving this list: planting climbers and perennials *before* infrastructure means a decade of working around plants you can't bear to move. This sequence is also why [[concepts/site-inventory]] should run before zoning, and zoning before any plant decision — backtracking is expensive once things are in the ground.
