# Paths-First Design

**Paths-first design** is the principle that paths, walkways, and other hardscape elements should be decided and placed *before* any beds are laid out or plants are selected. The paths define the skeleton of the garden; beds and plants fill the spaces between them.

Two independent commenters in the community thread arrived at this advice separately — one as a practical principle (Utretch), one as a six-step algorithm (philltheosopher). See [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]].

## Why it matters: decomposing the blank canvas

TopshelfMintTea names the psychological effect directly: "Once a walkway or gravel path is laid out it takes the giant canvas thing down to smaller spaces to work with" (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). A blank lawn feels paralyzing because it is undivided; every possible layout competes equally. A path cuts the canvas into distinct regions — each region now has its own character, constraints, and scale, and becomes tractable to design independently.

This maps directly onto [[concepts/garden-zoning]]: zones are functional rooms, and paths are the hallways between them. [[concepts/bubble-drawing]] is the iteration tool for trying different path and zone arrangements before committing to hardscape.

## Start from how you already move

Utretch notes: "I paid attention to the paths I most naturally took around the yard and noted to leave them open" (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). A second commenter ([deleted]) agrees: "using pathways (or the ways you already naturally walk through the space) as a starting point to the design."

The practical implication: before drawing anything, walk your property several times and notice where your feet naturally go. Those desire lines are the bones of your path system. Design that ignores them creates gardens that feel awkward to move through.

## A six-step sequence (philltheosopher)

The closest thing to an algorithm in the thread — presented here as-written because it's complete enough to follow (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]):

1. Walk the property. Figure out the easiest way to move around it. Note site features that help or hurt your effort.
2. Rough-sketch the property, including the windows of the house.
3. On top of your sketch, draw: (a) your paths, and (b) window-visibility regions. Goal: a garden you like walking around *and* can see from inside.
4. Pencil in where you want garden beds. For maintenance and access, keep the whole bed within about 5 feet of a path — so a bed with a path on both sides is about 10 feet wide. (This is negotiable; do what's best for you.)
5. Once beds are roughed in, walk the property again. Label each roughed-in bed with sun condition and drainage/moisture. That becomes the basis for species selection when you're ready.
6. Plan the phasing order — in what sequence will you build out the beds?

Note that step 5 is essentially a targeted [[concepts/site-inventory]] pass, applied to each defined bed. The full site inventory ideally happens before this sequence; if you've already done it, step 5 is just reading your notes back onto the bed sketch.

## Keyhole paths maximize reachable area

Utretch shapes his paths in keyhole form "so that most of the space is easily reachable without leaving the path" (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). A keyhole path penetrates into a bed and loops back — it converts a bed that would require stepping in for maintenance into one that's fully accessible from the path edge. This is especially useful in densely-planted native gardens where stepping into a matrix planting risks compacting soil or damaging low groundcovers.

## Softcorner buildings; use yard corners as backdrops

TopshelfMintTea: "Corners of buildings do nicely with ornamental shrubs to soften the corners. Corners of the yard do nicely with big stuff — takes the edge off of things from a distance. Then it provides a backdrop to fill things in." This is a supplementary heuristic that pairs with paths-first: once paths establish the basic skeleton, corners of structures and lot lines are natural anchor points for structural plants. See also [[concepts/planting-design-heuristics]] — the "structure first" heuristic for keystone plants.
