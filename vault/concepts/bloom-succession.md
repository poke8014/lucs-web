# Bloom Succession

**Bloom succession** is the design goal of having something in flower across every season — spring, summer, and fall at minimum. In mild climates like coastal or valley California, with careful species selection the bloom window can extend to near year-round.

This is an explicit planning goal, not an outcome that happens automatically. Most individual native plants bloom for a relatively short window; a planting that doesn't account for succession will peak in one season and look sparse or purely structural the rest of the year (see [[concepts/planting-design-heuristics]] — designing for off-bloom appearance).

## Why it matters

### Aesthetics
The thread commenters who discuss succession are primarily motivated by appearance: they want something interesting to look at from inside the house and while walking through the garden year-round. One gardener (lefence) explicitly checks each month's bloom coverage and color harmony before finalizing a plant list (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]).

### Pollinator forage
*This framing is the project's extension of the thread's argument — the thread argues aesthetics; the pollinator-forage angle is Sunshower's.* Continuous bloom means continuous forage: native bees and other pollinators need pollen and nectar from early spring through late fall. A garden that blooms only in summer leaves early-emerging bees (native bumblebees begin foraging in late winter in the Bay Area) and late-season migrants without reliable food. Designing for succession is therefore both an aesthetic and an ecological goal for this project.

## How gardeners actually verify succession before planting

These are the methods that appeared in the community thread — low-fi and tool-agnostic:

### "Season of show" spreadsheet column
toxicodendron_gyp's color-coded spreadsheet included a column for "season of show" alongside height, sociability, light needs, and water needs (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). Sorting by this column shows immediately whether the full plant list is front-loaded toward one season. This is one of the clearest use cases for a structured plant database over a visual diagram: season filtering is a query, not a drawing.

Plant pages in this vault carry `bloom_season:` frontmatter precisely for this query. A Sunshower selector that lets a gardener filter by bloom season — or flags gaps in their current selection — is the digital version of this spreadsheet column.

### Month-by-month visual storyboard
lefence made a Google Slides deck with one slide per month, each slide showing photos of what would be blooming that month (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). The goal was to check both coverage (is every month represented?) and color harmony (does the spring palette clash with the summer palette?). This is more effort than the spreadsheet column but gives a visual, feel-based check that a sorted spreadsheet can't.

### Pre-engineered kits
Waterfallsofpity's statewide arboretum sells plug sets explicitly designed for spring/summer/fall bloom plus native grasses — a guaranteed three-season succession built into the product (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). The heuristic for choosing a kit: look for explicit three-season coverage language, not just a plant list.

## Spring ephemerals as early-season gap fillers

lefence tucks spring ephemerals into gaps between plants where the space is bare early in the season. Ephemerals bloom before many perennials leaf out, occupy the light and moisture available in early spring, then go dormant by summer. They contribute meaningfully to the early-spring succession window without competing with summer plants. See [[concepts/planting-design-heuristics]] for the placement heuristic.
