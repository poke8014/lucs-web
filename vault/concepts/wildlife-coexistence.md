# Wildlife Coexistence

A garden is a habitat whether or not the gardener intends it to be. **Wildlife coexistence** is the operating stance of designing and maintaining the garden as a shared space rather than a sterile one — tolerating insects, spiders, snails, birds, mammals, and the food chain they form — and refusing the products (pesticides, herbicides, plastic turf, plastic "living walls") that strip a yard of life.

For a [[concepts/right-plant-right-place|native pollinator garden]] this is more than a stance; it's the *point*. The garden's value is measured in the pollinators and other wildlife it supports.

## Why it matters

Two reinforcing reasons surface across the beginner sources:

1. **The food chain is the system, not an obstacle.** Spiders eat insects. Birds eat spiders and slugs and aphids. Hedgehogs eat snails. Removing one trophic level — usually by killing insects — collapses the others.
2. **Wildlife-friendly gardens are *easier* to maintain.** Predators handle pest pressure for free if you let them establish. Bees pollinate vegetables and fruit trees. A "spotless" garden is a high-maintenance one because its natural balance has been removed.

The Sunshower project leans on this: the app's tone should be *supporting native plants and pollinators*, not *destroying invasives*. The plant-classification distinction (Cal-IPC invasive vs. common residential weed vs. native) lets us speak with calibrated urgency — Cal-IPC High invasives genuinely harm wildland ecosystems and deserve removal; many "common weeds" are minor enough that aggressive language is misleading.

## What this looks like in practice

### Tolerate the imperfect
- **Dandelions in the lawn?** Great for bees. Leave them.
- **A bare patch where the dog wees?** Acceptable. Real lawns recover.
- **Aphids on a rose?** Wait. Ladybugs and lacewings usually show up.
- **A snail track on a hosta leaf?** Not catastrophic. Hand-pick if numbers grow; otherwise leave.

### Skip the `-cides`
- **No pesticides.** Anything ending in *-cide* is killing something in a food chain. The collateral damage usually outweighs the target.
- **No herbicide as default.** For Cal-IPC invasives, herbicide may be necessary at last resort and with appropriate timing — see [[synthesis/invasive-removal-methods]] for the calibrated approach. For routine weeds, hand-pulling and mulching usually suffice.
- **No slug pellets** containing metaldehyde — they kill pets, birds, and the predators that would otherwise eat the slugs.

### Refuse the plastic shortcuts
- **No plastic grass.** Plastic turf provides no habitat, gets hot enough to burn pets, sheds microplastics, ends up in landfill, and is *less* low-maintenance than its marketing claims.
- **No plastic "living walls."** Same critique. A real climbing plant on a real fence (ivy, native vines) does the same visual job and supports actual life.

### Add habitat affirmatively
- **Trees** — even in a small yard. Habitat for birds, insects, fungi; shade for everything below.
- **A pond or water source** — for amphibians, drinking water for birds and pollinators. (Note safety considerations for households with small children.)
- **Log piles, brush piles, leaf litter** — habitat for ground beetles, salamanders, overwintering pollinators.
- **A "messy" corner** that the mower doesn't reach.

## A friendly tone, not an aggressive one

Beginner gardeners often arrive with the framing "what should I kill or remove?" The wiki — and Sunshower's user-facing copy — should consistently reframe to "what should I support, and how?" Removal still happens (for genuine invasives, for safety, to make room), but it's framed as *making space for what belongs*, not as *winning a war against weeds*.

This affects copy in [/sunshower/cleanup-plan](../../src/app/sunshower/cleanup-plan/page.tsx) and on the landing page — the cleanup phase still exists, but its purpose statement is the native pollinator garden that follows, not the dead weeds that came before.
