# Right Plant, Right Place

The principle that a plant's success — and its ecological contribution — depends on whether it is matched to the place it's planted.

This is the **thesis statement** of Sunshower. Every other concept in this wiki is, in some way, in service of helping a gardener answer: *what is the right plant for this place?*

## Two layers

The principle has a horticultural reading and an ecological reading. Both matter; the project takes the ecological reading as primary.

### Horticultural layer (necessary)

Match a plant's preferred conditions to the site's actual conditions:

- The right [[concepts/usda-hardiness-zones|hardiness zone]] for the plant to survive winter cold (and increasingly, summer heat).
- The right [[concepts/sun-requirements|sun exposure]] — full sun, part sun, or shade.
- The right [[concepts/soil-basics|soil texture and pH]] — sandy, clay, alkaline, acidic.
- The right water regime — drought-tolerant plants don't belong in irrigated lawn edges; thirsty plants don't belong on sunny dry slopes.
- Adequate [[concepts/plant-spacing|space]] for the plant's mature size.

This is the reading the generic gardening literature emphasizes. Authors like Pretty Purple Door's [[sources/flower-gardening-for-beginners|Amy Fedele]] frame it as: *amend less, choose better* — pick plants whose native conditions match what your yard already provides, instead of fighting the site to support the wrong plant.

If you only get this layer right, your plants will survive. They might even thrive.

### Ecological layer (the project's thesis)

A plant that survives is not necessarily the right plant. The ecological reading asks a harder question:

> Does this plant *belong* here, in the network of species that co-evolved with this place?

For California, the answer turns on **nativity**. Native pollinators — bees, butterflies, moths, beetles, hummingbirds — co-evolved with the native plants of their range. Many of those relationships are specific:

- A monarch caterpillar can only develop on milkweed (*Asclepias*). No milkweed in the garden, no monarchs from that garden.
- Many native bees are specialists, foraging on a single genus or family. Generalist non-native plants don't substitute.
- Co-evolved bloom timing means native pollinators emerge expecting native bloom — a yard full of non-native ornamentals can be a desert at the right time of year even if it looks lush.

A garden of thriving non-native ornamentals satisfies the horticultural layer. It fails the ecological layer. **For this project, "right plant" means native plant; "right place" means in the ecological community where it belongs.**

## Why this is the thesis

Sunshower exists because:

- Native pollinator populations are declining.
- A primary driver is loss of native plant habitat.
- Home gardens, in aggregate, are a meaningful share of land available for restoration.
- Most homeowners default to non-native ornamentals because that's what nurseries push and what generic garden advice teaches.
- The information needed to choose natives — what's local to your specific microclimate, what supports which pollinators, where to source it — is fragmented and hard to assemble.

The app's job is to make the ecological reading of "right plant, right place" the *easy* reading. A user shouldn't have to be an expert to reach the same answer an expert would.

## How this principle shapes the wiki

- **Plant pages** carry `nativity:` in frontmatter as a primary axis (`native | non_native_safe | invasive`). Non-native plants exist in the wiki for context and contrast, but native plants are the primary citizens.
- **Site-condition concepts** ([[concepts/site-inventory]], [[concepts/sun-requirements]], [[concepts/soil-basics]], [[concepts/usda-hardiness-zones]]) feed *into* this principle — they're the inputs that constrain which natives are appropriate for a given yard.
- **Pollinator pages** anchor the demand side: what each pollinator group needs, which specifically depends on which native plants.
- **Region pages** (when they exist) define what "native" means for a given location — California isn't one ecology; coastal sage scrub, oak woodland, chaparral, and Sierra foothills are different communities.
