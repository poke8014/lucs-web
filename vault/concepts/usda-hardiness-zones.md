# USDA Hardiness Zones

A standardized way to describe **how cold a region's winter gets**, used to predict whether a perennial plant will survive year-round in that region.

The USDA divides North America into zones numbered **1 (coldest) through 13 (warmest)**. Each zone represents a 10°F band of average annual minimum temperature. (Each zone is also commonly subdivided into "a" and "b" half-zones for finer 5°F resolution — e.g., 9a is colder than 9b.)

## How to read a zone tag

A plant labeled **"Zones 5-9"** can survive in zones 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9 — but is not reliably winter-hardy outside that range:
- **Colder than zone 5** → winter cold will kill it.
- **Warmer than zone 9** → it lacks the chill it needs (for many temperate species), or summer heat exceeds its tolerance.

If your zone falls inside the listed range, the plant should overwinter outdoors. If your zone is at the *edge* of the range, the plant becomes a "delicate" plant — see [[concepts/frost-and-cold-protection]].

## How to find your zone

- **USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map** (the canonical tool, updated periodically as the climate shifts).
- Many gardening sites have lookup-by-ZIP-code interfaces.
- Outside the US, search for your country's equivalent system or look up your "USDA-equivalent" zone — most international references will translate.

## Caveats

- **Zones describe winter cold only.** They say nothing about summer heat, humidity, sun intensity, soil, or drought. A plant rated for zones 7-9 in the Atlantic Southeast may behave very differently in zone 9 California, where summers are dry rather than humid.
- **The map updates.** Zones have shifted northward as climate warms. The 2012 map (referenced in the source) was updated in 2023; using an outdated map can give misleading "winter survival" guidance. Always check the most recent USDA release.
- **Microclimates can differ from the published zone.** A south-facing wall, a frost pocket at the bottom of a slope, or proximity to thermal mass (concrete, water) can make a yard half a zone warmer or colder than the regional reading.
- **Heat zones** exist as a separate system (the AHS Plant Heat Zone Map). For California gardens, summer heat tolerance is often the more limiting factor than winter cold.

## Relationship to other concepts

- A first-pass filter on plant selection alongside [[concepts/sun-requirements]], [[concepts/soil-basics]], and water needs — feeds into the broader [[concepts/right-plant-right-place]] match.
- For native CA plants, hardiness zones are a coarse instrument. **California-native plant communities are better described by ecoregion** (e.g., coastal sage scrub, chaparral, oak woodland) than by USDA zone alone. When `regions/` pages exist, they'll layer ecological community on top of zone.
