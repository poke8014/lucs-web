# Plant Strategy Systems (C-S-R and beyond)

Tools for translating "thousands of available species" into *usable design categories*, based on how plants survive in the wild rather than how they look. Rainer & West's [[sources/planting-in-a-post-wild-world|*Planting in a Post-Wild World*]] (Ch. 3) present three lineages of strategy system and then simplify them into the [[concepts/planting-layers|vertical-layer]] method. Understanding the strategies is what lets you fill each layer with compatible plants.

## Grime's C-S-R theory (the core)

British ecologist J. Philip Grime observed that plants face three pressures — **competition**, **stress** (drought, shade, low nutrients), and **disturbance** (fire, herbivory, mechanical damage) — and that a plant can only invest its resources in three directions, forcing a three-way trade-off:

- **C — Competitor.** Thrives where stress and disturbance are low and growing conditions are good. Wins by fast growth and out-competing neighbors. *Prairie plants, mesic-meadow species.* → often **structural** or theme plants.
- **S — Stress-tolerator.** Survives high-stress, low-disturbance sites by conserving biomass — slow growth, long-lived leaves, few seasonal changes. *Green-roof species, spring ephemerals.* → often **ground-cover** plants.
- **R — Ruderal.** Colonizes high-disturbance, low-stress ground fast — completes its life cycle quickly and dumps huge amounts of seed. *Many beloved annuals — and many dreaded garden weeds.* → the **filler** layer.

**Design use:** match the strategy to the site's dominant pressure. A high-traffic urban strip (constant disturbance) calls for R-heavy choices that self-repair after damage. **Limitation the authors name:** few plants are purely one type — most blend all three — so C-S-R is a strong *conceptual* model but gives little concrete plant-combining guidance on its own.

This is also a useful lens on Sunshower's weed work: the [[concepts/vegetative-spread|invasive]] plants in the cleanup-plan are overwhelmingly aggressive competitors or ruderals exploiting [[concepts/right-plant-right-place|disturbed ground]], which is why filling the ground densely (denying the niche) is the durable defense.

## Hansen's habitat method (the predecessor)

Richard Hansen & Friedrich Stahl (*Perennials and Their Garden Habitats*, 1979): if you plant species in conditions matching their **wild habitat**, they live longer and self-regulate — a "living ground cover" (*lebendige Bodendecke*). You can combine plants from *different* regions as long as they share a habitat type (a Mediterranean sage + California chaparral annuals + Eurasian scrubland grasses). This is essentially [[concepts/right-plant-right-place|right-plant-right-place]] at the community scale, and it's also where the [[concepts/plant-sociability|sociability scale]] originates. Limitation: Hansen's plant lists were Europe-specific and hard to translate regionally.

## Kühn's plant types (the practical blend)

German professor Norbert Kühn (*Neue Staudenverwendung*, 2011) merged Grime + Hansen and added a plant's morphology, spreading behavior, and temporal niche, narrowing to **8 garden-relevant types**:

| Type | Strategy | Examples | Fits layer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Conservative growth (slow, creeping, extreme sites) | *Lavandula, Santolina, Phlox subulata* | ground cover (dry) |
| 2 | Moderate stress adaptation | *Aquilegia, Hosta, Salvia nemorosa* | theme / ground cover |
| 3 | Stress avoidance (spring geophytes, go dormant) | *Helleborus, Crocus, Allium* | ground cover (ephemeral) |
| 4 | Area occupation (tall perennials, stand their ground) | *Rudbeckia fulgida, Phlox paniculata* | **structural / theme** |
| 5 | Area coverage (carpet-forming ground covers) | *Geranium sanguineum, Ceratostigma* | **ground cover** |
| 6 | Area expansion (aggressive clonal spreaders) | *Ajuga, Solidago rugosa, Eutrochium maculatum* | ground cover / matrix |
| 7 | Niche occupation (managed-meadow plants, re-flower if cut) | *Salvia pratensis, Colchicum* | theme |
| 8 | Gap occupation (short-lived, big seeders) | *Erigeron annuus, Digitalis, Gaura* | **filler** |

The practical payoff: a low-management planting might use Type-4 tall perennials as its design layer over a Type-5 ground-cover carpet, and it will largely run itself.

## How the book resolves all three

All three systems are plant-centric, all require deep plant knowledge, and none gives aesthetic guidance. Rainer & West extract the useful parts and re-frame everything as **[[concepts/planting-layers|vertical layers]]** added top-down — the strategy systems then help *fill* each layer with plants of appropriate behavior. For Sunshower, C-S-R and the Kühn types are most useful as **tags that predict which layer a native belongs in** and how it will behave under competition — a checker's inputs, not a generator's.
