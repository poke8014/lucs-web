# Planting Design Heuristics

A set of recurring layout rules of thumb that appeared across multiple commenters in a native-gardening community thread (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). These are horticultural community wisdom — not formally researched, single-source — but they surfaced independently from several experienced gardeners, which gives them practical weight.

These heuristics operate at the plant-arrangement level. They follow from completing the [[concepts/site-inventory]] and deciding on [[concepts/garden-zoning]]; they're applied *while* placing plants within a bed, not before.

## Height layering

**Tall in back (or center of an island bed), medium in the middle, lowest in front.** This is the most-cited rule in the thread — mentioned by the top-voted commenter (robsc_16, 68 pts), Utretch (18 pts), and others (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]).

**Exception: wispy "see-through" tall plants can sit forward.** lefence notes that some tall plants are "wispy that you can see through" and don't block sightlines even from a front position. The prairie-plant vocabulary includes many such species — tall grasses and upright perennials with open, airy structure. In California, many native grasses fill this role well. The rule is really about *visual blocking*, not height alone.

## Group in odd numbers; plant in drifts

**Group plants in threes to fives (sometimes sevens), and plant drifts of a few species rather than one each of many.** robsc_16 recommends "groups of 3–5." LucChak articulates the logic: "You don't want 1 of 20 different plants, you want a triangle of 3 plants and 6 different triangle varieties" (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]). A drift of the same plant reads as intentional; a single specimen of each of twenty species reads as a collection.

## Repeat plants and colors for cohesion

**Repeat the same plants and colors in multiple locations through the garden.** lefence explicitly names this: "I repeated the same colors and plants throughout the garden a few times to make it look cohesive." For a front yard with a more formal character, repetition is the primary tool for visual unity across a planting that might otherwise look random.

## Always include native grasses and sedges

This appears in at least three independent comments (robsc_16, Utretch, Waterfallsofpity, lefence). Grasses and sedges provide structure in the off-season, movement in the wind, and habitat value (nesting material, overwintering cover) that flowering perennials alone don't supply. In California, native sedges (*Carex* spp.) are especially useful in part-shade and seasonally moist spots; native grasses (*Muhlenbergia*, *Nassella*, *Danthonia*) anchor meadow-style beds.

## Layered / matrix planting

toxicodendron_gyp describes a matrix/vignette/structure vocabulary: plugs at 12-inch centers into a low matrix of sedges, grasses, and groundcovers, with mid-layer vignette plants above and occasional tall structure plants. Mature-size circles overlap by design at this density — which is why graphical planning breaks down (overlapping circles on paper look like chaos). The spreadsheet approach handles this better than a spatial diagram (see [[sources/reddit-native-garden-plan]]).

This approach is aligned with the Rainer/West "Planting in a Post-Wild World" framework (community-endorsed; see source page). In California contexts, a matrix of native bunchgrasses or low *Carex* with native perennial vignettes above is a direct translation.

## Tuck spring ephemerals into gaps

lefence places spring ephemerals in gaps between plants "where it is bare earlier in the season." This is a density and timing strategy: ephemerals use the growing space and light that's available before other plants leaf out, then go dormant and leave room. Directly relevant to [[concepts/bloom-succession]] — ephemerals extend effective bloom earlier into the year without competing with summer plants.

## Structure first: start with keystone plants

Coffee_24-7 recommends: start with a foundation/keystone plant — oak is best, then redbud or crabapple, then any large shrub — and build outward. Soften building corners with shrubs; use big plants in yard corners as backdrop. This is the *architecture-before-furnishings* principle applied to gardens. The tree or large shrub anchors the composition before any perennial is placed.

The eastern examples (oak, redbud) are species-specific. In California, native oaks (*Quercus agrifolia*, *Q. lobata*, *Q. douglasii*) are the strongest keystone species; large native shrubs (*Ceanothus*, *Heteromeles*, *Sambucus*) can substitute where oaks are impractical. The principle — structure plant first — transfers directly.

philltheosopher's 6-step workflow complements this: he places paths before plants, then uses sun/drainage labels on each bed as the species-selection basis. See [[concepts/paths-first-design]].

## Design for off-bloom appearance

TopshelfMintTea makes the point plainly: "Natives are tough to work with in that respect because they don't bloom for a long time. You may want to consider what they look like when not in bloom." This is not a criticism of natives — it's a planning requirement. The heuristic: for each plant in the plan, ask what it looks like for the 10 months it is *not* in peak bloom. Grasses and sedges (above) are a partial answer; structural plants (evergreen shrubs, interesting bark) are another. See [[concepts/bloom-succession]] for the positive framing — designing for continuous sequential bloom so there's always *something* interesting.
