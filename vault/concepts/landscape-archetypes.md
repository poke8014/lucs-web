# Landscape Archetypes

Before choosing any plants, a [[concepts/designed-plant-communities|designed plant community]] starts with a **goal landscape** — a simple, universal wild reference that sets the mood, structure, and signature patterns the planting will evoke. Rainer & West's [[sources/planting-in-a-post-wild-world|*Planting in a Post-Wild World*]] (Ch. 2–3) reduce the world's vegetation to four archetypes and give a key for reading which one a site "wants to be."

> A designed plant community is held together by an *idea* — the goal community — chosen before the plants themselves. "See in your site the wild beating heart that wants to be expressed."

## The four archetypes

- **Grassland** — all-herbaceous, open, no trees/shrubs. Reads as meadow/prairie. Can be short or tall, wet or dry, flower-rich or a calm sea of grasses.
- **Woodlands & shrublands** — scattered trees/shrubs with open canopy and space between them. Layered, tiered heights.
- **Forest** — closed tree canopy, open visually beneath the trunks; ground plane of ephemerals, sedges, ferns, mosses.
- **Edge** — the transition between two of the above (grassland edge, woodland/forest edge). Often the right pick for suburban lots — screens a road, blends open and wooded areas.

## The landscape selection key

The book gives a dichotomous key (like a plant ID key), driven by the *existing woody structure* visible on and around the site:

1. **Trees and shrubs absent, all herbaceous** → **grassland**
2. **Trees and shrubs present**
   - **Open canopy, space between trees** → **woodlands & shrublands**
   - **Closed canopy of trees** → **forest**

The point is to read the site's given structure rather than impose an unrelated fantasy on it (a Mediterranean garden in the mid-Atlantic "works" only with unlimited budget and vision; most sites already contain a fragment of character to build on).

## Reading a site: exploration before analysis

The authors deliberately downplay the data-heavy McHarg-style inventory (topography/hydrology/soils spreadsheets) in favor of **qualitative observation first**:

- **Wander the site aimlessly** and note emotional pulls — what draws you (a vista, a sheltered spot → focal point) and what repels you (a dank thicket → remove, or amplify as contrast).
- **Squint and zoom out** to see past distracting details (flowers, ornaments, existing paths) to the "bones": tree cover, surface, water flow.
- **Sketch as a way of seeing** — plan view for canopy/patterns, section/perspective for vertical layers. New sites are often easier to read than familiar gardens (memory clouds judgment).
- **Be willing to clear** — but never clear more than you can replant (bare disturbed soil invites weeds; see [[concepts/management-not-maintenance]]).

This enriches the vault's existing quantitative [[concepts/site-inventory]] framework with a qualitative front-end: *feel the site's archetype first, measure second.*

## Urban / disturbed sites

Even a rooftop or paved plaza has an archetype hiding in it, read mostly from **light and soil depth** rather than existing vegetation: an exposed sunny roof → grassland/meadow; a courtyard boxed by tall buildings → forest floor. The rule: *almost any urban problem-site has a parallel wild community* (brownfields ≈ deep-rooted grasslands that absorb metals; plazas ≈ rock outcrops; biofilters ≈ stream-bank edges).

## One site, one idea (usually)

Commit to a **single archetype** where possible — blending too many reads as cluttered. A single grassland can still hold internal variety (wet floriferous meadow at a slope bottom, short grass on a ridge). Larger sites with genuinely distinct conditions can carry multiple goal communities, but the transitions between them then become the hard design work.

## Why this matters for Sunshower

- The archetype is the natural **top of the per-section planning flow**: pick a section's goal community (meadow / woodland-edge / etc.), and it constrains the whole downstream palette and [[concepts/planting-layers|layer]] mix. This corroborates the community-sourced "base map → site inventory → paths → sectioning → *then* plants" sequence.
- California's native plant communities (from the Calscape `native.communities` field — chaparral, coastal scrub, oak woodland, valley grassland…) are the CA-specific vocabulary that plugs into these four coarse archetypes. That's the bridge from this general theory to a native palette.
- The selection key is simple enough to encode as an app step.
