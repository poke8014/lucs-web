# Planting Layers

The operational heart of a [[concepts/designed-plant-communities|designed plant community]]: instead of laying out plants in single-species blocks, you build the planting as a set of **vertical layers** stacked on top of one another in space and time. Density and diversity come from this stacking — *not* from cramming plants close together. From Rainer & West's [[sources/planting-in-a-post-wild-world|*Planting in a Post-Wild World*]] (Ch. 3).

## Design layers vs. functional layers

The four layers split into two families, and this split is the book's mantra — **"legibility in the design layers, diversity in the functional layers."**

- **Design layers** — the tallest, most visually dominant plants. They form your *impression* of the landscape. Their job is aesthetic and legible; keep them relatively simple and readable.
- **Functional layers** — the low, ground-holding plants almost nobody notices. Their job is to cover soil, suppress weeds, control erosion, feed pollinators, build soil. Here you want *maximum diversity* — intermingle several similar species and no one will notice, but the insects find more food and the planting is more resilient.

German designer Heiner Luz's motto captures it: *"uniformity in the large scale, variety in the small scale."*

## The four layers

Percentages are of total plant quantity in the mix, per the book's summary table.

| Layer | Family | % of planting | Role | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Structural / framework** | Design | 10–15% | The backbone/silhouette of the planting; carries it year-round | Trees, dominant shrubs, tall architectural grasses & perennials. Distinct silhouettes, **long-lived, clump-forming**. Tend to be competitors or stress-tolerators. |
| **2. Seasonal theme** | Design | 25–40% | Visually dominates for a season (bloom or texture), then recedes to green companion | Mid-height perennials in masses/drifts. Medium longevity. Amorphous shapes that spill around the structural plants. |
| **3. Ground cover** | Functional | ~50% | Holds the ground, fills every gap between taller plants — "green mulch" | Low, shade-tolerant, often rhizomatous/clonal spreaders. Sedges, low forbs, ephemerals. The workhorses. |
| **4. Filler** | Functional | 5–10% | Temporary cover while slow structural plants establish; self-seeding insurance that repairs gaps after disturbance | Annuals, biennials, short-lived perennials (Grime **ruderals**). Big seed producers; disappear once the community closes. |

### Layer 1: Structural / framework
Choose for **longevity, clump-forming (not running) habit, and year-round structure**. Avoid short-lived hybrids (Echinacea, Coreopsis cultivars) and aggressive runners (Physostegia, Pycnanthemum) here — they erode the frame's clarity. Note the trap: long-lived structural plants (e.g. Baptisia) are often *slow to establish*, channeling energy underground for years before showing much above ground — so use larger, more established stock for this layer and don't panic when they "sit there."

### Layer 2: Seasonal theme
Used in quantity, so exact placement of each plant matters less — think in **bold sweeps and drifts**. Connect their bloom colors to the characteristic colors of the wild reference community (yellow prairie in fall, blue floodplain forest in spring) to strengthen the evocation. If theme plants vanish or multiply too much, the whole planting suffers — pick medium-vigor, medium-life species.

### Layer 3: Ground cover
The layer most often *missing* from traditional plantings, and the one that makes a planting a true community. Selection follows the *functional need*: erosion control wants evergreen persistent basal foliage; storm water wants deep diverse roots; soil-building wants legumes. The authors' lament — good native ground covers are often not commercially available "because they're not ornamental enough," so designers substitute available low shade-tolerant sedges/perennials. **A standing wish: native equivalents of Vinca, Pachysandra, and English ivy** (aggressive but *non-invasive* ground covers).

### Layer 4: Filler
Distribute evenly so they can self-seed throughout. They act as "inbuilt insurance": e.g. cardinal flower (*Lobelia cardinalis*) disappears once a rain garden closes, but its banked seed re-germinates the moment a contractor disturbs the soil years later. In California terms, this is exactly how native annual wildflowers behave.

## Laying it out (installation order)

Plant top-down, one layer at a time (Ch. 4):
1. **Place structural plants carefully** — fewest in number, most visually load-bearing.
2. **Arrange seasonal theme plants** as sweeps/drifts — less fussy, higher quantity.
3. **Fill in ground cover** between and under everything — place a *population*, not individuals; a grid doesn't matter, even coverage does.
4. **Add fillers + bulbs** into the gaps; bulbs can be arranged semi-independently since they don't compete much.

## Why this matters for Sunshower

- The layer model is a natural **plant-categorization axis** for the app — a native plant can be tagged by which layer(s) it can play, and a per-section plan can be checked for "does this section have a ground-cover layer, or is it 100% showy mid-height plants over bare soil?" (the most common failure mode). See [[synthesis/post-wild-world-and-sunshower]].
- The 50% ground-cover target is a concrete, checkable heuristic — closer to a plan *checker* than a generator, which matches the demonstrated demand from [[concepts/right-plant-right-place|community sources]].
- It reframes [[concepts/plant-spacing]]: density is a layering property, not a spacing number.
