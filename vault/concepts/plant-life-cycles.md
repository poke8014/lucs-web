# Plant Life Cycles

The classification of flowering plants by how many growing seasons they live and reproduce. Determines what to expect after the first year, how much replanting work the garden needs, and (importantly for pollinators) how reliable bloom timing is from year to year.

Maps directly to the `plant_type:` field on plant pages.

## Three categories

### Perennial

A plant that lives **multiple years**. Most return each spring after dying back (or losing leaves) over winter. Trees and shrubs are also typically perennial — even if they're not what most people picture when they say "perennial flower."

- **Garden implication:** plant once, return each year. Lower-maintenance over time.
- **Pollinator implication:** stable, predictable bloom in the same place year after year. Native pollinators that depend on a specific bloom can rely on it.

### Annual

A plant that completes its full life cycle — germinate, grow, flower, set seed, die — **in a single year**. After flowering it dies entirely; the same plant does not return next spring.

- **Garden implication:** must be replanted every year.
- **Why use them:** annuals typically bloom longer and more prolifically than perennials, so they fill gaps in color, shape, or pollinator forage during a season.

### Biennial

A plant that lives **two years**: in year 1 it grows leaves only (often a basal rosette), in year 2 it flowers, sets seed, and dies. Common examples include foxglove and many wildflowers.

- **Garden implication:** the plant you set out as a seedling will not flower until next year. Plan for staggered planting if you want bloom every year.

## Self-sowing annuals — the gray area

Some annuals produce abundant seed before dying, and those seeds germinate the following spring without intervention. Functionally the population persists year after year, even though no individual plant is perennial.

- Common in many native CA wildflowers (poppies, lupines, clarkias) — drop seed in summer, germinate in fall/winter rains.
- Helpful for low-maintenance native gardens; can be disruptive in a curated bed where the gardener wants tighter control.

## Why this matters for the project

- **Plant frontmatter:** `plant_type` (perennial / annual / shrub / tree / etc.) is a structured field on every plant page.
- **Pollinator support:** native pollinators benefit most from *predictable, recurring* forage, which favors perennials and self-sowing natives. A garden of high-bloom annual cultivars can look great but provides less stable habitat.
- **User expectations:** an app recommendation that says "plant this" implies different commitments depending on life cycle. The UX should make that explicit.
