# Planting Technique

The mechanical act of putting a plant in the ground. Beginner-grade content covered well by generic horticulture sources; CA-native-specific planting (especially timing for fall planting before winter rains) will warrant its own page when a CA-native source addresses it.

## Planting depth

The single most consequential mechanical choice. Get this wrong and the plant struggles for the rest of its life.

- **Rootball flush with surrounding soil.** The top of the rootball should sit level with the soil grade around it.
- **Too deep** — buried trunk flares, buried crowns. Trees planted too deeply rarely thrive; they often die slowly over years. Crown rot kills shrubs.
- **Too shallow** — rootball "proud" of the soil surface. Dries out fast, root tips desiccate, plant struggles or dies.

Before planting, **weed and prepare the soil** in the planting area. A young plant rooted into a bed of weeds loses the competition every time.

## "Start small" — the cardboard-suppression trick

When taking on a new bed, an allotment, or any larger area, beginners commonly tackle the whole thing at once and burn out. The pragmatic alternative from [[sources/gardenersworld-beginner-tips]]:

> Cover the areas you don't want to cultivate yet with **cardboard or black plastic** to stop weeds from growing while you work on a different area.

The principle scales to any new gardener: pick one bed, do it well, suppress the rest passively while you work. The suppressed areas wait quietly until you have capacity. Mulching over the cardboard makes it visually neutral while it does the suppression work.

This pairs with Sunshower's [parallel-tracks idea](../../planning/sunshower/CONTEXT.md#ideas-surfaced-from-beginner-gardening-ingest-2026-05-15): a user can be **site-inventorying or planning** at the kitchen table while the cardboard does its slow work outdoors, and **actively removing weeds** in the bed they're currently developing. The "do everything at once" trap is the failure mode the cardboard avoids.

## Plant last — work logically

From [[sources/littleterracedhouse-designing-a-garden]]: when transforming a yard, plants come last in the construction sequence. Before planting, finish:

1. Clearing unwanted features (old patios, plastic grass, fences).
2. Hardscape and lighting (electricians, patios, paths, pergolas).
3. Furniture and large fixtures (sheds, dining areas).
4. Border shapes.
5. **Then plants.**

The regret-driver: planting climbers on fences you're about to replace, or perennials in the path of a patio you're about to lay, is expensive to undo and emotionally hard to redo.

This sequencing is most relevant for *transforming* a yard (new homeowner, major renovation). For maintenance planting in an established garden, the principle still holds at smaller scale: don't plant against a structure you're about to change.

## Maintenance after planting

- Water consistently during the establishment period — see [[concepts/watering]]. Newly planted perennials and shrubs need regular water until roots reach into surrounding soil; trees and large shrubs need consistent watering for the first year or two even if drought-tolerant.
- Mulch around the plant (but not piled against the stem/trunk) — moderates soil temperature and moisture, suppresses weeds.
