# Horticultural Introduction Pathway

A large fraction of California's invasive plants entered the state as **deliberately-imported ornamentals**, escaped cultivation, and naturalized into wildlands. The horticultural trade is the single most-cited introduction pathway in [[sources/calipc]] Plant Assessment Forms.

This is not a story about "a few bad apples." It's a structural feature of how the nursery trade has operated: showy, low-care, drought-tolerant non-natives are exactly the traits that make a successful invader, and exactly what sells. Cal-IPC's 2004 nursery survey documented many invasive plants still being actively sold at California nurseries decades after their wildland impacts were known.

## How the pathway works

1. Plant is imported as ornamental — often valued for low-water tolerance, vigorous growth, dense ground coverage, or rapid screening.
2. Sold and planted in residential and municipal landscapes statewide.
3. Plant escapes cultivation via:
   - Wind-borne or animal-dispersed seed
   - Runoff carrying tissue or seed downstream
   - **Dumped landscape debris** along roadsides and waste areas (a major route for vegetative spreaders — see [[concepts/vegetative-spread]])
   - **Intentional re-planting** in wildlands by people who like the plant
4. Naturalized populations expand. The plant becomes a wildland problem.
5. By the time the wildland impact is documented, the plant is established in nursery catalogs and home landscapes — making source-side prevention politically and commercially difficult.

## Worked example

[[plants/arctotheca-prostrata]] (capeweed) entered California horticulturally as an ornamental groundcover. Its PAF documents:

> "Introduced for horticulture. Still widely used in landscaping. Can be spread by mechanical equipment along roads. ... Found in Cal-IPC nursery survey 2004."

> "Some individuals have also been known to plant this weed intentionally in wildland areas."

The trajectory — imported as ornamental → still sold → escaping cultivation → being re-planted by enthusiasts in wildlands — is repeated across many entries in the Inventory.

## Why it matters for a CA pollinator garden

The thesis here is [[concepts/right-plant-right-place]] — natives in their native context. The horticultural pathway is the *anti-pattern*: a non-native chosen because it looked good at the nursery, with no consideration of ecological fit. Even when the homeowner's intent is benign, the plant they bring home may be the next wildland invader.

Practical implications:

- **Verify before planting.** Cross-check candidate ornamentals against the Cal-IPC Inventory and PlantRight's "Don't Plant a Pest" lists.
- **Buy from native-focused nurseries.** California Native Plant Society chapter sales, native plant nurseries, and regional reservations pre-filter for ecological fit.
- **Avoid the "cheap, vigorous, drought-tolerant" trap.** Those traits in a non-native often correlate with invasiveness.
- **Be skeptical of "sterile cultivars."** Sometimes the plant being sold as "sterile" is the form most actively spreading vegetatively (capeweed is a textbook case — sterile capeweed spreads by stolons; the "sterile" labelling addresses seed dispersal only).
