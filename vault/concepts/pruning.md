# Pruning

Selective cutting of branches, stems, or roots to shape a plant, control its size, encourage flowering / fruiting, or maintain health. Pruning intimidates beginners more than it should — done correctly the rewards (better growth, more flowers, more fruit) far outweigh the risk.

From [[sources/gardenersworld-beginner-tips]]:

> Pruning plants can seem like a daunting job, but if you learn how to do the job properly, you'll be rewarded with plants that look good, grow well, and they're likely to flower and fruit better, too. The key to successful pruning is to know **when to prune** and **follow guidance on how to make the cuts** and shape the plant.

## Two questions to answer per plant

1. **When?** Pruning timing varies dramatically by plant. Many flowering shrubs are pruned right after flowering; many fruit trees are pruned in winter dormancy; some pollinator plants are left standing through winter for overwintering invertebrates. *The single biggest beginner mistake is pruning at the wrong time of year.*
2. **How?** Cut placement (above a node or bud, at the correct angle, just outside the branch collar for trees), tool choice (clean cuts with sharp tools), and amount removed per session (rule of thumb: ≤⅓ of the plant in one go for most species).

This stub doesn't develop either question. The source provides only the meta-advice that pruning is plant-specific and reference-able. Per-species pruning timing belongs on individual plant pages once that level of detail is captured.

## For pollinator gardens specifically

A wildlife-conscious nuance from [[concepts/wildlife-coexistence]]: **leave standing stems and seedheads through winter** for many perennials. Overwintering native bees, lacewings, and other beneficial insects use hollow stems for shelter, and seedheads feed birds. The typical "tidy up everything in fall" advice runs counter to this; the better default for pollinator gardens is to delay cutback until early spring.
