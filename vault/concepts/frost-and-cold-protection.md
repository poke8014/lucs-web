# Frost and Cold Protection

When to plant in spring (so cold doesn't kill new growth), how to handle plants whose hardiness sits at the edge of your zone, and how to cover plants when an unexpected cold snap hits.

## Last-frost date

The **average date of the last spring frost** in a region. Planting earlier than this date risks losing tender new transplants to a cold night, even if those plants would otherwise survive in the local [[concepts/usda-hardiness-zones|hardiness zone]].

- **Even cold-hardy perennials** can be killed when planted too early — established plants tolerate cold better than fresh transplants.
- Lookup tools: **Farmers' Almanac frost-date tool**, NOAA climate data, county agricultural extension records. They give a date plus a probability (e.g., "10% chance of frost after April 15").

For California:
- **Coastal zones** rarely frost at all; last-frost dates are largely irrelevant.
- **Inland valleys and foothills** do experience spring frost; last-frost timing varies by elevation and microclimate.
- **High-elevation Sierra zones** have late frosts well into May.

## "Delicate" plants on zone edges

A plant rated for zones 6-9 planted in **zone 6** (the cold edge of its tolerance) needs more care than the same plant in zone 8. Pretty Purple Door calls these "delicate" plants. Two strategies:

1. **Site placement** — choose a microclimate warmer than the published zone:
   - South-facing walls (thermal mass radiates heat, reflects sun).
   - Areas sheltered from cold wind.
   - Near concrete, brick, water — anything that holds and releases warmth.
2. **Active protection** — cover the plant during cold nights (see below).

The inverse case — a plant on the *warm* edge of its range — is just as common in California. A plant rated zones 5-8 planted in zone 9 may struggle from heat or insufficient winter chill, not cold. The sources ingested so far don't develop this; worth its own treatment when corroborated.

## Covering plants for a cold snap

If an unseasonably cold night threatens after planting:

- **Frost cloth** (purpose-made garden fabric) — breathable, designed to hold ground warmth around the plant.
- **Improvised covers** — old sheets, blankets, burlap draped over stakes to form a tent. Plastic alone is risky; if it touches the plant the contact points often freeze.
- **Cover before sundown** — the goal is to trap daytime ground heat under the cover. Covering after the air is already cold doesn't help much.
- **Remove the cover in the morning** once temperatures rise so the plant doesn't overheat or stay too humid.
